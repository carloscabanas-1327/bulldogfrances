What is the best food for a French Bulldog? Not a single brand, but a food that meets clear standards. Here are the questions veterinary nutritionists recommend asking.

Key takeaways

  • Look for a "complete and balanced" statement for your dog's life stage on the label.[2]
  • WSAVA suggests asking the company eight questions, including who formulates the food and what quality control it uses.[1]
  • "Grain-free," "natural" and "premium" are marketing terms, not proof of better nutrition.
  • Home-cooked diets need a recipe from a board-certified veterinary nutritionist.

Start with "complete and balanced"

In the US, pet food labels carry a nutritional adequacy statement. AAFCO explains that "'complete' means the product contains all the nutrients required" and "'balanced' means the nutrients are present in the correct ratios."[2] A food can meet that standard in two ways:

  • Formulation: the recipe is designed to meet AAFCO nutrient profiles.
  • Feeding trials: the food was fed to dogs under AAFCO procedures and shown to provide complete and balanced nutrition.[2]

Make sure the statement matches your dog's life stage: growth (puppies), adult maintenance, or all life stages. Products labeled as treats or snacks usually are not meant to be complete and balanced.[2]

The WSAVA questions to ask a pet food company

The World Small Animal Veterinary Association suggests these questions:[1]

  1. Do they employ a qualified nutritionist?
  2. Who formulates the diet?
  3. What quality control do they use for ingredients and finished products?
  4. What product research or nutrition studies have they done?
  5. Does the label have a nutritional adequacy statement?
  6. How many calories per gram or serving?
  7. Does the company give a phone number or email for questions?
  8. Who actually makes the food?

WSAVA's advice: "If the manufacturer cannot or will not provide any of this information, veterinarians and owners should be cautious about feeding that brand."[1]

What about grain-free food?

Grain-free diets became popular, but there is no evidence that grains are bad for most dogs. The FDA has investigated reports of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), a heart disease, in dogs eating certain diets, many of them grain-free. Its most recent update was in December 2022, and the agency says it "does not intend to release further public updates until there is meaningful new scientific information to share."[3] Talk to your vet before choosing a grain-free food.

Frenchie-specific things to consider

  • Kibble size and shape. A short jaw can make large kibble hard to pick up and chew. Small, flat pieces often work better.
  • Calorie density. Frenchies gain weight easily. A food with moderate calories lets you feed a satisfying portion.
  • Skin and digestion. French Bulldogs are prone to allergic skin disease. If your dog itches year-round, ask your vet about a food trial rather than switching brands repeatedly.[4]
  • Eating speed. Gulping can lead to regurgitation. A slow-feeder bowl can help.

Home-cooked and raw diets

Home-cooked meals sound healthy, but recipes found online are often missing key nutrients like calcium. If you want to cook, ask your vet to refer you to a board-certified veterinary nutritionist for a recipe. Raw diets also carry a risk of bacteria for both the dog and the people in the home.

Switching foods

Change foods gradually over about a week, mixing in more of the new food each day. Then reassess body condition after a few weeks, since calorie content varies between brands.
