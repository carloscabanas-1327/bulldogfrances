What is the best food for a French Bulldog? Evaluate nutritional adequacy, manufacturer evidence and your dog's health—not marketing claims.[2]

Key takeaways

  • Look for a "complete and balanced" statement for your dog's life stage on the label.[1]
  • WSAVA provides questions about qualified formulators, quality control, published research, nutritional adequacy and contact information; it does not certify specific commercial brands.[2]
  • Labels such as "grain-free" or "premium" alone do not establish diet safety, nutritional quality or suitability. Assess the complete product and the manufacturer's evidence.[2]
  • Home-cooked diets need a recipe from a board-certified veterinary nutritionist.

Start with "complete and balanced"

In the US, pet food labels carry a nutritional adequacy statement. AAFCO explains that "'complete' means the product contains all the nutrients required" and "'balanced' means the nutrients are present in the correct ratios."[1] A food can meet that standard in two ways:

  • Formulation: the recipe is designed to meet AAFCO nutrient profiles.
  • Feeding trials: the food was fed to dogs under AAFCO procedures and shown to provide complete and balanced nutrition.[1]

Make sure the statement matches your dog's life stage: growth (puppies), adult maintenance, or all life stages. Products labeled as treats or snacks usually are not meant to be complete and balanced.[1]

The WSAVA questions to ask a pet food company

The World Small Animal Veterinary Association suggests these questions:[2]

  1. Do they employ a qualified nutritionist?
  2. Who formulates the diet?
  3. What quality control do they use for ingredients and finished products?
  4. What product research or nutrition studies have they done?
  5. Does the label have a nutritional adequacy statement?
  6. How many calories per gram or serving?
  7. Does the company give a phone number or email for questions?
  8. Who actually makes the food?

WSAVA advises caution if a manufacturer will not supply essential formulation and quality information.[2] Its questions are evaluation criteria, not an endorsement or a ranking of Royal Canin, Purina, Hill's or any other brand.

What about grain-free food?

The FDA continues to study possible diet-associated non-hereditary dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM). Reports have involved both grain-free and grain-containing foods, often with peas or other pulses high on ingredient lists. The FDA says it lacks definitive evidence that these diets are inherently unsafe; reports do not prove that a specific ingredient causes DCM.[3][5] Consult your veterinarian, especially for dogs with cardiac signs or on unusual diets.

Frenchie-specific things to consider

  • Kibble size and texture. Choose a form the individual dog can comfortably pick up and eat. The cited evidence does not prove that a particular shape is best for all French Bulldogs.
  • Calorie density. Compare calories per serving and the dog's body condition. A lower-calorie food is not automatically the best choice for puppies, active dogs or those losing weight unintentionally.
  • Skin and digestion. French Bulldogs are prone to allergic skin disorder. If your dog itches year-round, ask your vet about a food trial rather than switching brands repeatedly.[4]
  • Eating speed and regurgitation. A slow feeder may help some fast eaters, but repeated regurgitation is a veterinary problem and should not be treated with equipment alone.

Home-cooked and raw diets

Home-prepared diets can be nutritionally incomplete unless formulated for the dog's needs. Ask for a recipe from a board-certified veterinary nutritionist and monitor the diet over time. Raw animal products can expose both people and dogs to pathogens; do not equate "natural" with microbiological safety.

Switching foods

Change foods gradually over about a week, mixing in more of the new food each day. Then reassess body condition after a few weeks, since calorie content varies between brands.
