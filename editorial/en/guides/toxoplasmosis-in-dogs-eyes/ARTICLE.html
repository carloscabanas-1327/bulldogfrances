Toxoplasmosis in dogs is usually thought of as a cat problem that dogs rarely notice. A small study published in 2026 by veterinary eye specialists at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem suggests the parasite behind it, Toxoplasma gondii, may also cause serious eye inflammation in dogs, sometimes ending in blindness. Here is what the researchers found, what it means for your French Bulldog, and which eye signs should send you to the vet.

Key takeaways

  • Israeli veterinary ophthalmologists described five dogs with eye disease and positive blood tests for Toxoplasma gondii, seen between 2023 and 2025.[1][2]
  • Problems ranged from inflammation at the front of the eye to retinal detachment and blindness; one eye had to be removed.[2][3]
  • All five dogs improved at first with treatment, but two relapsed.[2]
  • Five cases cannot prove cause or risk factors. The study is a signal for vets, not a reason to panic.[2][3]
  • Sudden vision loss is an emergency: see a vet the same day.[7]

What is toxoplasmosis in dogs?

Toxoplasmosis is an infection with the single-celled parasite Toxoplasma gondii. Wild and domestic cats are the only animals in which it can complete its life cycle, and they are the main source of infection for other animals and for people.[4] Infected cats pass the parasite's eggs (oocysts) in their feces.[5]

Other warm-blooded animals, dogs included, can pick it up in two main ways: by eating undercooked meat that contains tissue cysts, or by swallowing food or water contaminated with cat feces that carry oocysts.[5]

Most of the time nothing happens. Healthy adult animals with a strong immune system usually control the infection and show no signs.[4] The picture changes in puppies and in adults with a weakened immune system, which are at high risk of sudden, severe illness.[4]

The 2026 study: five dogs with eye disease

The paper, titled "More than a feline disease: ocular manifestations in five dogs infected with Toxoplasma gondii," appeared in Veterinary Parasitology: Regional Studies and Reports in September 2026.[1][2] It was led by Dr. Yamit Soueid of the ophthalmology team at the Koret School of Veterinary Medicine, Hebrew University of Jerusalem, with Prof. Itamar Aroch, and the university announced it on November 8, 2026.[2]

The team looked back through the records of dogs treated at the university's Veterinary Teaching Hospital from January 2023 to December 2025. To be included, a dog needed eye disease, a positive blood test for antibodies (IgG) against the parasite, at least one follow-up blood test and at least one eye recheck.[3]

Five dogs met those rules. They were 3.5 to 8 years old; four were mixed breeds and one was a Weimaraner.[3] In three of them, the antibody level rose fourfold on the repeat test, a pattern that points to an active infection.[2][3]

Toxoplasmosis in dogs' eyes: what the vets saw

The eye problems were not mild. According to the case summary:[3]

  • Two dogs had anterior uveitis (inflammation inside the front of the eye) in both eyes.
  • Two dogs had panuveitis (inflammation of the whole eye) in one eye, with retinal detachment and blindness in that eye.
  • One dog had panuveitis in both eyes with partial retinal detachment and went suddenly blind.

One blind eye later developed glaucoma and was surgically removed; the release says that dog was six years old.[2][3]

The Weimaraner, eight years old, first had fever, lethargy and uveitis in both eyes. About three months after its antibiotic course ended, it came back with severe inflammation of the eyelids (blepharitis). The researchers say this may be the first reported link between severe blepharitis and suspected eye toxoplasmosis in a dog.[2][3]

Several dogs had other health problems or had received drugs that dampen the immune system. In the published summary these included steroid treatment for an immune disorder that destroys platelets, leishmaniasis, and allergic skin disease treated with oclacitinib.[3] That fits what is known in other species: a weaker immune system makes serious toxoplasmosis more likely.[4]

Treatment and outcome

All five dogs received the antibiotic clindamycin by mouth plus anti-inflammatory eye drops, and all of them improved at first.[2][3] The dog that had gone blind in both eyes regained its sight within two weeks, although lasting changes in the retina were seen later.[2][3]

Two dogs relapsed despite long treatment and kept high antibody levels.[2] In the Weimaraner, two alternative drugs had to be stopped because of side effects.[3] Only a veterinarian can choose and dose these medicines; never use leftover antibiotics or eye drops on your own.

What the study does not show

This is a small, backward-looking case series, and the authors are clear about its limits:[2][3]

  • A positive antibody test alone does not prove the parasite caused the eye disease.
  • Five dogs are too few to identify risk factors, breeds or how common this is.
  • The diagnosis relied on IgG titers and response to treatment; IgM testing was not available locally.

To confirm cases in the future, the authors suggest tests on fluid from inside the eye or on tear samples.[3] The study also does not say how these dogs became infected. Dr. Soueid's message is aimed at vets: greater awareness matters "particularly when treating dogs with unexplained eye inflammation."[2]

What it means for French Bulldog owners

No French Bulldogs were among the five dogs, and nothing in this study suggests the breed is at special risk of toxoplasmosis.[3] But Frenchies already have more than their share of eye trouble, so it pays to know the warning signs. See our guides to red eyes in French Bulldogs and a French Bulldog going blind.

See a vet the same day if your dog suddenly seems unable to see, or has a red, painful or cloudy eye. Sudden vision loss is an emergency.[7] Tell the vet if your dog takes steroids or other immune-suppressing medicines, or has a chronic illness. Our guide to blindness in dogs explains other causes.

Simple habits reduce exposure to the parasite for the whole household:

  • Wash hands, utensils and surfaces after handling raw meat, and cook meat to at least 152.6 °F (67 °C).[4]
  • Freezing meat for several days at 0 °F (−18 °C) before cooking greatly reduces the chance of infection.[6]
  • If you also have a cat, clean the litter box daily and feed the cat only cooked or commercial food.[4]
