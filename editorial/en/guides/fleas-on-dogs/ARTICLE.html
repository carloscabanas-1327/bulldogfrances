Fleas on dogs cause more than itching: they can trigger skin allergies, make young puppies anemic and pass tapeworms. Getting rid of them is very doable, but only if you know one thing most owners don't. The fleas you see on your dog are a small fraction of the problem; most of the infestation is living in your house.

Key takeaways

  • Adult fleas on the pet are only about 5% of the population. The other 95% are eggs, larvae and pupae in the home.[1][2]
  • To clear fleas, treat every dog and cat in the household, plus the house itself.[3][4]
  • US parasite experts recommend flea prevention year-round, not just in summer.[4]
  • Many dog flea products contain permethrin, which is highly toxic to cats.[6]

What fleas on dogs look like (and how to check)

The flea found on most dogs in the US is actually the cat flea, Ctenocephalides felis.[3] Adults are tiny, dark brown and fast. Slowly part the hair, especially over the hips, the base of the tail and the thighs, and look for moving fleas or black specks.[5]

Those specks are "flea dirt" (flea feces made of digested blood). To confirm, the wet paper towel test: comb the specks onto a damp white paper towel. Flea dirt dissolves into a reddish brown smear.[5] Ordinary dirt stays black.

A French Bulldog's short coat makes fleas easier to spot than on a fluffy dog. Still, not finding fleas doesn't rule them out: a flea-free exam does not rule out flea allergy, and an allergic dog can react strongly to very few fleas.[7]

Symptoms and health risks

  • Scratching, chewing and red, crusty skin. Flea allergy dermatitis is a reaction to flea saliva. Classic signs are crusted, itchy skin over the hips, the base of the tail and the thighs.[5] The real test is whether the itch improves with strict flea control.[7] That makes fleas worth ruling out first in any itchy Frenchie.
  • Tapeworms (Dipylidium caninum). Dogs get them by swallowing fleas while grooming. Children can also be infected by swallowing an infected flea.[3][4]
  • Anemia from heavy infestations, especially in young puppies. Pale gums or weakness in a flea-covered puppy need a vet the same day.[3]
  • Diseases in people. Cat fleas can transmit Bartonella henselae and some rickettsial infections, including murine typhus.[3]

Why fleas keep coming back

A female flea starts laying eggs within a day or two of her first blood meal and can lay up to 40 to 50 eggs a day.[3] The eggs fall off your dog onto the floor, the bed and the couch.

StageTypical length[3]
Egg1 to 6 days
Larva5 to 11 days (up to 2 to 3 weeks)
Pupa (cocoon)1 to 2 weeks, but can wait up to 30 weeks
Full cycle in a homeUsually 3 to 8 weeks

Pupae wake up when they sense vibration and warmth from a passing host. That is why fleas seem to "appear" in large numbers when you come home from vacation or pick up your dog from boarding; the fleas weren't necessarily from the kennel.[8]

Fleas on dogs treatment: a 3-step plan

  1. Treat every pet with an effective product. Ask your vet for a flea preventive suited to each dog's and cat's weight and age. Flea shampoos and powders kill fleas at the time you use them but have little lasting effect.[8]
  2. Treat the house. Wash your dog's bedding in hot water, vacuum often (carpets, couch cushions, cracks, under furniture) and empty the vacuum outside. In heavy infestations, a household spray combining an adult flea killer with an insect growth regulator is used and repeated after 7 to 10 days.[3]
  3. Keep going. You will still see some fleas for weeks as pupae keep hatching. VCA advises continuing flea control on all pets for 3 to 6 months after an infestation.[8] In flea-allergic dogs, complete elimination can take up to 3 months.[7]

"The flea treatment isn't working." According to VCA, there is no evidence of fleas developing resistance to insecticides. Failures usually come from incorrect application, untreated pets or rooms, or pupae still hatching.[8]

Home remedies such as vinegar, lemon sprays or essential oils don't reach the eggs and pupae hiding in your carpet, which make up most of the population.[1]

Young puppies: not every product is safe for small puppies. Each label lists a minimum age and weight; ask your vet for one that fits. More on early care in our French Bulldog puppy care guide.

Fleas in the house and on people

Can fleas live on humans? Fleas do bite people, and adult fleas will jump onto people looking for a meal.[5][8] But they infest pets, not people: the life cycle runs on your dog or cat and in your home.[5] People can, however, carry fleas indoors on clothing and infest unprotected pets.[3] That is how indoor-only dogs get fleas.

Year-round prevention. The Companion Animal Parasite Council (CAPC) recommends treating all dogs and cats year-round, for life, so fleas never establish in the home.[4] Central heating and carpets let fleas breed indoors even in winter.[8] A good routine: monthly preventive, a quick comb-through after baths (see how often to bathe a French Bulldog), and an ear check while you're at it, since many preventives also cover ear mites.

If you also have cats

Permethrin is found in many dog spot-ons, collars, sprays and shampoos. Cats can't break it down. Poisoning happens when a dog product is put on a cat, or when a cat grooms or sleeps against a recently treated dog. Signs include drooling, tremors and seizures, and untreated poisoning is usually fatal.[6] If your cat shows these signs after contact with a dog flea product, it is an emergency: go to the vet now. Always read the label and ask your vet which product is safe for a mixed household.
