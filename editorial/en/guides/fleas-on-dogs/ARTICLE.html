Fleas on dogs may trigger intense itching, allergic dermatitis, tapeworm infection and anemia in heavily infested puppies. Effective control treats every pet with a species-appropriate product and addresses flea development in the home.[4]

Key takeaways

  • Adult fleas on the pet are only about 5% of the population. The other 95% are still developing in the home as eggs, larvae and pupae.[1][2]
  • To clear fleas, treat every dog and cat in the household, plus the house itself.[3][4]
  • US parasite experts recommend flea prevention year-round, not just in summer.[4]
  • Many dog flea products contain permethrin, which is highly toxic to cats.[5]

What fleas on dogs look like (and how to check)

Most fleas found on dogs are actually cat fleas, Ctenocephalides felis.[3] Slowly part the hair and look for fast-moving fleas or black specks.[6]

Those specks are "flea dirt" (flea feces made of digested blood). To confirm, the wet paper towel test: comb the specks onto a damp white paper towel. Flea dirt dissolves into a reddish brown smear.[6] Ordinary dirt stays black.

A French Bulldog's short coat may make fleas easier to spot than on a fluffy dog. Still, not finding fleas doesn't rule them out: very sensitive flea-allergic dogs can be almost free of fleas.[7]

Symptoms and health risks

  • Scratching, chewing and red, crusty skin. Flea allergy dermatitis is a reaction to flea saliva. Classic signs are crusted, itchy skin over the hips, the base of the tail and the thighs.[6] The real test is whether the itch improves with strict flea control.[7] That makes fleas worth ruling out first in any itchy Frenchie.
  • Tapeworms (Dipylidium caninum). Dogs get them by swallowing fleas while grooming. Children can also be infected by swallowing an infected flea.[3][4]
  • Anemia from heavy infestations, especially in young puppies. Pale gums, marked weakness or collapse in a heavily infested puppy can signal severe anemia; seek emergency veterinary assessment rather than waiting for a routine appointment.[3]
  • Diseases in people. Cat fleas can transmit Bartonella henselae and some rickettsial infections, including murine typhus.[3]

Why fleas keep coming back

A female flea starts laying eggs within a day or two of her first blood meal and can lay up to 40 to 50 eggs a day.[3] The eggs fall off your dog onto the floor, the bed and the couch.

StageTypical length[3]
Egg1 to 6 days
Larva5 to 11 days (up to 2 to 3 weeks)
Pupa (cocoon)1 to 2 weeks, but can wait up to 30 weeks
Full cycle in a homeUsually 3 to 8 weeks

Vibrations from walking and carbon dioxide from breathing trigger new fleas to come out of their cocoons. That is why fleas seem to "appear" in large numbers when you come home after a long time away or pick up your dog from boarding; the fleas weren't necessarily from the kennel.[8]

Fleas on dogs treatment: a 3-step plan

  1. Treat every pet with an effective product. Ask your vet for a flea preventive for each dog and cat in the home. Flea shampoos and powders kill fleas at the time you use them but have little lasting effect.[8]
  2. Treat the environment. Wash washable bedding, vacuum carpets and upholstery repeatedly and dispose of vacuum contents safely. Severe infestations may require a licensed pest professional; follow environmental pesticide labels and keep pets away until re-entry instructions are met.[3]
  3. Keep going. You will still see some fleas for weeks as pupae keep hatching. VCA advises continuing flea control on all pets for 3 to 6 months after an infestation.[8] Fully clearing an established infestation from the home often takes up to 3 months.[7]

"The flea treatment isn't working." Fleas can reappear when pets or areas are untreated, doses are missed or pupae continue to emerge. Do not assume the product has failed or increase a dose without discussing the problem with a veterinarian.[8]

"I bathed my dog and the fleas came back." Shampoos and powders only kill the fleas on your dog at that moment.[8] About 95% of the flea population is still growing up in your home.[1]

Young puppies: not every product is approved for every age or weight. Ask your veterinarian which products fit your pet's age, size and medical history. The FDA warns that isoxazoline-class flea and tick drugs have been associated with neurological reactions in some pets, though most use them safely.[9] More on early care in our French Bulldog puppy care guide.

Fleas in the house and on people

Can fleas live on humans? Fleas do bite people, and adult fleas will jump onto people looking for a meal.[6][8] But they infest pets, not people: the life cycle runs on your dog or cat and in your home.[6] People can, however, carry fleas into the home and infest unprotected pets.[3] That is how indoor-only dogs get fleas.

Year-round prevention. The Companion Animal Parasite Council (CAPC) recommends treating all dogs and cats year-round, for life, so fleas never establish in the home.[4] Indoors, flea larvae develop in carpets and other sheltered spots.[4] A good routine: prevention at the product's labeled interval (monthly for many, but not all products), a quick comb-through after baths (see how often to bathe a French Bulldog), and an ear check while you're at it, since many preventives also cover ear mites.

If you also have cats

Some dog-only flea products contain permethrin, which can cause severe and sometimes fatal poisoning in cats. A cat may be exposed if given a dog product or allowed close contact with a dog before the product is fully dry, according to its label. Warning signs include drooling, tremors and seizures.[5] If your cat shows these signs after contact with a dog flea product, it is an emergency: go to the vet now. Always read the label and ask your vet which product is safe for a mixed household.
