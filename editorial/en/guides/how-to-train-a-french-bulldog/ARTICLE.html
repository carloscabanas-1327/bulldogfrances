How to train a French Bulldog comes down to three things: short sessions, rewards your dog actually wants, and a lot of consistency. Frenchies are smart and eager for attention, but they have a reputation for being stubborn, and harsh methods tend to make them dig in. Here is what the evidence says works, from puppy basics to biting and leash walking.

Key takeaways

  • The AKC says French Bulldogs "can be stubborn, so they may need more training than other breeds".[1]
  • Veterinary behaviorists (AVSAB) recommend reward-based methods for all dog training and advise against force, pain or fear.[2]
  • In a study of 92 dogs, those trained with aversive methods showed more stress behaviors and higher cortisol.[3]
  • Keep sessions to 5 to 10 minutes, and walk your Frenchie on a harness instead of a collar.[4][7]

Are French Bulldogs easy to train?

Somewhat. The AKC describes Frenchies as "smart, social, and affectionate", but also warns that they "can be stubborn, so they may need more training than other breeds".[1] In practice, "stubborn" often means a Frenchie has decided the reward isn't worth the effort, or the session went on too long. Make it easy to succeed, make the payoff good, and stop while your dog is still keen.

Two physical traits matter too. Frenchies overheat easily and tire quickly, so train indoors or in the shade, keep sessions short, and stop before your dog is panting hard.

Why reward-based training works

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) states that "evidence supports the use of reward based methods for all canine training" and that "there is no evidence that aversive training is necessary". It links aversive methods with more anxiety, fear-related aggression and a reduced ability to learn.[2]

Research backs this up:

  • A 2020 study in PLoS ONE compared 92 dogs from seven training schools. Dogs from schools using aversive methods showed more stress behaviors (lip licking, yawning), larger rises in cortisol after training, and a more "pessimistic" outlook in a thinking test.[3]
  • A survey of 364 owners found that obedience was linked to training with rewards, not punishment, and that punishment was linked to more behavior problems.[5]

The RSPCA sums it up simply: "All training should be reward based."[6]

How to train a French Bulldog puppy

You can start the day your puppy comes home, usually at around 8 weeks old.

  • Short sessions: 5 to 10 minutes, focused on one thing, ending with a game. If your puppy struggles, go back a step instead of forcing it.[4]
  • Tiny treats: pea-sized pieces your puppy can eat fast. Count them as part of the day's food, because extra weight is hard on a Frenchie's breathing. Our guide to how much a French Bulldog should eat helps you set the daily amount.
  • Start with the basics: name, come when called, sit, stay, and drop it.
  • One set of rules: everyone in the house uses the same cue words and rewards the same things.

The two biggest early projects get their own guides: how to potty train a French Bulldog and puppy socialization. Both are easiest in the first weeks at home.

How to train a French Bulldog not to bite

Puppies explore with their mouths, and the chewing stage lasts until about 6 months of age.[8] Nipping is normal, but you can steer it:

  1. When your puppy mouths hands or clothes, pause play for a few seconds.
  2. Offer a chew toy instead, and praise your puppy for using it.
  3. Make sure your puppy gets enough sleep. Overtired puppies often bite more.
  4. Never hold the muzzle shut, tap the nose or hit. These teach a puppy to fear hands.

Biting in an adult dog, or growling over food and toys, is a different issue. See our guides to food aggression in dogs and French Bulldog aggression.

Leash training with a harness

For a French Bulldog, a harness is a better choice than a collar. Pressure on the neck can make breathing harder for a flat-faced dog. One study also found that pulling on a collar raised pressure inside the eye, while a harness did not.[7]

To teach loose-leash walking:

  • Reward your dog often for walking next to you with a slack leash.
  • When the leash goes tight, stop and wait. Move on when it loosens.
  • Practice somewhere boring first, like a hallway or yard, before busy sidewalks.
  • Keep walks short in hot or humid weather.

House training basics

"How to train a French Bulldog not to pee in the house" is one of the most common questions owners ask. The short answer is timing plus rewards. The Royal Kennel Club says puppies usually pee within about 15 minutes of a meal and poop within about 30, and need to go out at least every hour or two.[11] Take your puppy out after waking, eating and playing, reward it the moment it goes in the right spot, and supervise closely indoors.

If you find an accident later, just clean it with an enzymatic cleaner. The ASPCA notes that a dog "will not learn anything by being scolded for a past accident".[10] If a dog that was reliably house-trained starts having accidents, check with your vet before treating it as a training problem.

What to avoid

  • "Alpha" or "pack leader" training. AVSAB advises against trainers who use dominance theory. Many dogs that growl are afraid, not trying to dominate.[2][9]
  • Physical punishment, yelling, or prong and shock collars. These raise fear and can lead to aggression.[2]
  • Rubbing your dog's nose in accidents. The ASPCA says this can make your dog fear you and teaches it to hide when it needs to go.[10]
  • Long sessions in the heat. Frenchies tire and overheat quickly.

If your dog struggles when you leave the house, read can French Bulldogs be left alone. For serious problems such as bites, ask your vet for a referral to a board-certified veterinary behaviorist or a certified trainer who uses reward-based methods.
