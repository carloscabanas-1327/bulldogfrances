Train a French Bulldog with short, reward-based sessions and consistent cues. Avoid punishment, and adapt exercise and training to the dog's breathing and comfort.

Key takeaways

  • The AKC says French Bulldogs "can be stubborn, so they may need more training than other breeds".[1]
  • Veterinary behaviorists (AVSAB) recommend reward-based methods for all dog training and advise against force, pain or fear.[2]
  • In a study of 92 dogs, those trained with aversive methods showed more stress behaviors and higher cortisol.[3]
  • Keep sessions brief and comfortable for your dog; 5–10 minutes is a practical suggestion, not a clinically established duration. A well-fitted harness can reduce neck pressure.[4][5]

Are French Bulldogs easy to train?

Training ease varies between dogs and contexts. The AKC's description of occasional "stubbornness" is breed guidance, not a clinical diagnosis or proof that French Bulldogs need more repetitions than another breed.[1] Apparent reluctance can also reflect stress, pain, fatigue or an unclear cue. Make it easy to succeed, make the payoff good, and stop while your dog is still keen.

Two physical traits matter too. Frenchies overheat easily and tire quickly, so train indoors or in the shade, keep sessions short, and stop before your dog is panting hard.

Why reward-based training works

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) states that "evidence supports the use of reward based methods for all canine training" and that "there is no evidence that aversive training is necessary". It links aversive methods with more anxiety, fear-related aggression and a reduced ability to learn.[2]

Research backs this up:

  • A 2020 study of 92 dogs at seven training schools reported more stress-related behaviors and physiological differences among dogs trained with aversive methods. The study did not test French Bulldogs as a breed or establish a precise harm rate for an individual dog.[3]
  • A survey of 364 owners found that obedience was linked to training with rewards, not punishment, and that punishment was linked to more behavior problems.[6]

The RSPCA sums it up simply: "All training should be reward based."[7]

How to train a French Bulldog puppy

You can start the day your puppy comes home, usually at around 8 weeks old.

  • Short sessions: start with just a few comfortable minutes on one skill and adjust to your dog's attention, breathing and body language. If your puppy struggles, go back a step instead of forcing it.[4]
  • Tiny treats: pea-sized pieces your puppy can eat fast. Count them as part of the day's food, because extra weight is hard on a Frenchie's breathing. Our guide to how much a French Bulldog should eat helps you set the daily amount.
  • Start with the basics: name, come when called, sit, stay, and drop it.
  • One set of rules: everyone in the house uses the same cue words and rewards the same things.

The two biggest early projects get their own guides: how to potty train a French Bulldog and puppy socialization. Both are easiest in the first weeks at home.

How to train a French Bulldog not to bite

Puppies explore with their mouths, and mouthing may continue as they mature; no exact age marks the end of nipping for every dog.[8] Nipping is normal, but you can steer it:

  1. When your puppy mouths hands or clothes, pause play for a few seconds.
  2. Offer a chew toy instead, and praise your puppy for using it.
  3. Make sure your puppy gets enough sleep. Overtired puppies often bite more.
  4. Never hold the muzzle shut, tap the nose or hit. These teach a puppy to fear hands.

Growling, snapping, bite injuries or guarding require a safety assessment. Do not remove food or toys by force or rehearse risky handling exercises. See our guides to food aggression in dogs and French Bulldog aggression.

Leash training with a harness

A well-fitting harness helps avoid concentrated neck pressure. A small study measured higher intraocular pressure during collar tension than during harness use; it did not establish that every harness fits every dog or directly measure prevention of airway disease.[5]

To teach loose-leash walking:

  • Reward your dog often for walking next to you with a slack leash.
  • When the leash goes tight, stop and wait. Move on when it loosens.
  • Practice somewhere boring first, like a hallway or yard, before busy sidewalks.
  • Keep walks short in hot or humid weather.

House training basics

House training uses timing, supervision and rewards. The Royal Kennel Club gives approximate post-meal and routine intervals, but they are not universal physiological deadlines.[9] Take your puppy out after waking, eating and playing, reward it the moment it goes in the right spot, and supervise closely indoors.

If you find an accident later, just clean it up. The ASPCA notes that a dog "will not learn anything by being scolded for a past accident".[10] If a dog that was reliably house-trained starts having accidents, check with your vet before treating it as a training problem.

What to avoid

  • "Alpha" or "pack leader" training. AVSAB advises against trainers who use dominance theory. Many dogs that growl are afraid, not trying to dominate.[2][11]
  • Physical punishment, yelling, or prong and shock collars. These raise fear and can lead to aggression.[2]
  • Rubbing your dog's nose in accidents. The ASPCA says this can make your dog fear you and teaches it to hide when it needs to go.[10]
  • Long sessions in the heat. Frenchies tire and overheat quickly.

If your dog struggles when you leave the house, read can French Bulldogs be left alone. For serious problems such as bites, ask your vet for a referral to a board-certified veterinary behaviorist or a certified trainer who uses reward-based methods.
