Distemper in dogs is one of the most serious viral diseases a dog can get. It attacks the respiratory, digestive and nervous systems, and no medicine kills the virus. The good news is that it is almost entirely preventable with the core puppy vaccine series, which is why distemper is the first shot to get right when you bring home a French Bulldog puppy.

Key takeaways

  • Canine distemper is caused by a highly contagious virus that affects the lungs, gut and nervous system.[1][2]
  • Puppies 2 to 6 months old and unvaccinated dogs are infected most often.[3]
  • There is no cure for the virus itself; care is supportive, and many dogs die or are left with lasting nerve damage.[2][4]
  • The vaccine is core: at least 3 doses between 6 and 16 weeks, a booster within a year, then every 3 years, per AAHA.[5]

How do dogs get distemper?

The virus spreads mainly through the air, in droplets from a sick dog's coughing, sneezing or barking, and also through saliva, urine and feces.[4] Two things make it hard to contain:

  • Dogs can spread the virus for several days before they look sick.[4]
  • Shedding usually lasts about a month, and some dogs with nervous-system signs shed for 6 to 8 months.[4] Dogs can keep shedding even after they seem to have recovered.[1]

In the US, wildlife matters too. Raccoons, skunks, foxes, coyotes, wolves and ferrets can carry distemper, and pet dogs can catch it from them.[2] Keep trash secured and don't let your dog approach wildlife that seems sick or unusually tame.

Distemper in dogs symptoms timeline

Distemper doesn't follow an exact calendar, but it tends to move through these stages:

WhenWhat you may see
3 to 6 days after infectionA first fever, often unnoticed.[1]
Early illness (signs often appear about 5 to 7 days after infection)[3]Eye discharge (often the first sign), fever of 103 to 105 °F (39.5 to 41 °C), runny nose, lethargy, poor appetite.[2][3]
Respiratory and digestive stageA cough that can turn from dry to wet if pneumonia develops, vomiting and diarrhea.[3]
SkinThickened, hardened footpads and nose ("hardpad disease").[1]
Weeks later (or 1 to 3 weeks after acute signs)Muscle twitching, "chewing-gum" seizures, head tilt, circling, or paralysis starting in the hind legs. Once these appear, the outlook gets worse.[1][6]

Distemper teeth: puppies that survive can be left with permanently pitted, discolored adult teeth from damaged enamel.[3]

Keep perspective: a single runny eye doesn't mean distemper. The AVMA notes that a weepy eye alone doesn't confirm the disease.[2] What should worry you is the combination of eye and nose discharge, fever and lethargy in a puppy that hasn't finished its shots. That is a same-day vet visit. Seizures are an emergency.

Can dogs survive distemper? Treatment

Some do. Not every infection is severe: the World Organisation for Animal Health notes that about half of infections in dogs and their wild relatives are mild or show no signs.[6] But the AVMA calls distemper "often fatal," and dogs that recover can have permanent nervous-system damage.[2]

There is no drug that kills the virus, and Cornell says antivirals are not recommended.[2][4] Treatment is supportive and usually needs a clinic:

  • IV fluids for dehydration
  • Antibiotics for secondary bacterial infections such as pneumonia
  • Medicines to control vomiting and seizures
  • Nutrition support

For dogs with severe, progressive disease, euthanasia may be discussed.[4] Don't try to wait it out at home, and be wary of "miracle cures" sold online. If you suspect distemper, keep your dog apart from other dogs and call the clinic before you arrive so staff can protect other patients.

Distemper vaccine schedule

Distemper is part of the core combination shot every dog should get, usually labeled DHPP or DAPP in the US (distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, with or without parainfluenza).[5] The 2022 AAHA guidelines recommend:[5]

  • Puppies: at least 3 doses between 6 and 16 weeks of age, given 2 to 4 weeks apart. WSAVA stresses that the last puppy dose should be at 16 weeks or older.[7]
  • Booster: one dose within 1 year of the last puppy shot. WSAVA suggests giving it earlier, at or after 26 weeks.[7]
  • Adults: boosters every 3 years.
  • Adult dogs never vaccinated (or first vaccinated after 16 weeks): 2 doses 2 to 4 weeks apart.

Why so many puppy shots? Antibodies from the mother block the vaccine during the first weeks of life, and no one can predict exactly when they fade in a given puppy.[7] The series covers that window.

Until the series is done, avoid places with lots of unknown dogs, but don't skip socialization; it matters just as much at this age. Is distemper contagious to humans or cats?

Humans: no. Canine distemper does not infect people.[3]

Cats: pet cats get a different disease often called "feline distemper" (panleukopenia), which is caused by feline parvovirus. The AVMA warns it is not to be confused with canine distemper.[8] Canine distemper does affect other wild species, including raccoons, skunks, ferrets and even some big cats in zoos and the wild.[2][6]

After a case of distemper at home

The virus doesn't last long outside the body, usually no more than a few days, though it survives longer in cold conditions.[3][6] Clean and disinfect bedding, bowls and floors with diluted bleach (about 1 part to 30 parts water) and leave it on for more than 10 minutes.[6] Before bringing a new dog home, make sure it is fully vaccinated and ask your vet about timing.
