French Bulldog hemivertebrae are abnormally formed vertebrae associated with the breed's naturally short or twisted tail. They can be incidental findings or contribute to spinal disease. In one CT study, 93.5% of 62 French Bulldogs examined for unrelated reasons and without neurological signs had hemivertebrae; this selected sample does not establish prevalence in the whole breed.[2] Learn which changes require veterinary attention.

Key takeaways

  • The Frenchie's short tail is genetic, not docked. A DVL2 gene variant associated with the screw-tail trait was found fixed in the French Bulldogs sampled in a 2018 study; that finding does not establish the genotype of every living dog.[1]
  • In a selected CT sample of 62 French Bulldogs without neurological signs, 93.5% had at least one hemivertebra. It is not a population-wide estimate.[2]
  • Many vertebral malformations do not produce symptoms, but severe spinal curvature (kyphosis) can be clinically significant and may be associated with disc disease.[3]
  • Progressive weakness or wobbliness in the back legs of a young dog needs a vet visit; sudden paralysis is an emergency.

Do French Bulldogs have tails?

Yes. French Bulldogs are born with a naturally short tail; it is not cut or docked. The AKC breed standard describes it as "either straight or screwed (but not curly), short, hung low, thick root and fine tip."[4] So both a short straight tail and a kinked "corkscrew" tail fit the standard.

The short, kinked tail has a known genetic basis. A 2018 study found a variant in the DVL2 gene that was the strongest genetic match for screw tail. Researchers reported the variant as fixed in French Bulldogs and English Bulldogs in the dogs they studied, and at high allele frequency in Boston Terriers. The variant was associated with the screw-tail phenotype in the examined dogs.[1] The genetic association helps explain linked traits; tail shape alone does not show whether the spinal cord is compressed.

What are hemivertebrae?

A hemivertebra literally means "half vertebra": one of the bones of the spine does not form completely.[3] Merck lists hemivertebrae alongside block vertebrae (fused bones) and butterfly vertebrae (split bones), and notes that hemivertebrae are "most common in screw-tailed dog breeds."[5]

Because the bone is only partly formed, the spine can bend at that point, most often into a hump called kyphosis.[3]

How common are French Bulldog hemivertebrae?

A 2017 retrospective study reviewed CT scans of 62 French Bulldogs without neurological signs that had been scanned for unrelated reasons:[2]

  • 93.5% had at least one hemivertebra; the sample was not a random survey of all French Bulldogs.
  • The middle of the chest (the 9th and 10th thoracic vertebrae) was most often affected.
  • Among 10 neurologically affected dogs selected for that study, one was a French Bulldog and nine were Pugs. Those counts cannot establish breed-wide lifetime risks.

A separate prospective German study of 265 brachycephalic dogs, including 199 French Bulldogs, found an association between severe tail malformations and vertebral malformations elsewhere in the spine. Tail shape by itself is not a diagnostic test.[6]

The Royal Veterinary College sums it up: in French Bulldogs, hemivertebrae with kyphosis usually do not cause signs, but they "should not be considered an innocent and benign condition." The curve slightly changes how these dogs walk and increases their risk of disc disease in the mid-back (IVDD), which is "by far the most common spinal disorder in French bulldogs."[3]

Hemivertebrae symptoms in French Bulldogs

When a malformation does press on the spinal cord, the RVC describes "progressive paresis and ataxia of the pelvic limbs" (gradually worsening weakness and wobbliness in the back legs), typically starting early in life.[7] In a selected referral-center group of 343 French Bulldogs with confirmed neurological disorders, 19 had a compressive vertebral malformation. Seventeen of those 19 had hemivertebrae, usually accompanied by severe kyphosis. These figures do not measure prevalence in all French Bulldogs.[8] Watch for:

  • A young dog that walks more clumsily, crosses or drags its back feet, or scuffs its nails
  • Back legs that get weaker over weeks or months
  • A visible hump in the back
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control

Emergency: if your Frenchie suddenly cannot stand, drags its legs or seems to have no feeling in its back paws, go to an emergency vet now. In this breed that is more often a disc herniation than a hemivertebra, and both need fast imaging. See back pain and weak legs in French Bulldogs.

Diagnosis: hemivertebrae on X-ray, CT and MRI

Most vertebral malformations are visible on ordinary X-rays.[9] They are often found by chance: in one CT study, almost all French Bulldogs scanned for other reasons had at least one.[2] A malformation seen on imaging does not, by itself, call for treatment.[9]

  • CT shows bone shape in detail and helps a surgeon judge whether a defect can be corrected.[5]
  • MRI is used when a dog has neurological signs, to see whether and where the spinal cord is compressed.[9]

The hardest part is deciding whether a malformation is relevant. The RVC notes it is "challenging to differentiate between clinically relevant and irrelevant vertebral malformations,"[3] so a neurological exam matters as much as the images.

Hemivertebrae treatment and surgery

An incidental hemivertebra finding without signs does not automatically mean surgery is necessary. A veterinarian should interpret imaging and advise on activity and follow-up. Our guide to French Bulldogs and stairs covers the evidence on jumping and stairs.

For dogs with progressive signs, the RVC reports that medical management alone "is unfortunately associated with a poor outcome," while surgery to stabilize the spine, with or without partial realignment, has given good results.[7] This is specialist surgery, usually planned from CT scans. Physical rehabilitation can help with strength and function.[9] Ask a board-certified veterinary neurologist or surgeon about the options for your dog.

Other French Bulldog tail problems

A tightly screwed tail often sits in a deep skin fold, the "tail pocket," which can trap moisture and get infected. Redness, smell or your dog scooting or licking the area are reasons to check it. Our guide to the French Bulldog tail pocket explains cleaning and when to see a vet. Researchers have proposed considering severe tail malformations in breeding decisions, but tail appearance cannot replace individual health assessment.[6]
