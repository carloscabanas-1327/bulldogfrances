A Frenchton is a cross between a French Bulldog and a Boston Terrier. It is a designer cross rather than an AKC-recognized breed, and reliable population-level lifespan data for Frenchtons are limited. What we do know comes from its two parent breeds, and both are flat-faced, screw-tailed dogs with well-documented health risks.

Key takeaways

  • A Frenchton is a mixed breed. The AKC offers mixed breeds an enrollment program, not breed registration.[1]
  • Both parent breeds may carry genetic variants associated with screw tails and vertebral malformations; inheritance and symptoms in an individual Frenchton cannot be predicted from breed labels alone.[2]
  • In a 2024 clinical sample, Boston Terriers more often had a BOAS grade of 0 than French Bulldogs in comparison data. This does not measure breathing outcomes in Frenchtons.[3]
  • Crossbreeding does not guarantee better health: a 2024 study of popular "designer" crosses found no difference from the parent breeds for most disorders.[4]

What is a Frenchton?

"Frenchton" is the name sellers use for a puppy with one French Bulldog parent and one Boston Terrier parent, or for later generations of those crosses. Because it is a cross, it would fit in the American Kennel Club's Canine Partners program, which the AKC describes as "an enrollment program for mixed breeds", not in breed registration.[1]

Because there is no standard, there is no "correct" Frenchton size, coat or look. Puppies in the same litter can look more like one parent or the other.

Frenchton vs Frenchie: how the parent breeds compare

The two parent breeds look alike, which is part of why the cross is popular. Their breed standards give a rough idea of the size range you might expect:

How big do Frenchtons get? The parent breeds' standards provide context, but a Frenchton can fall outside those ranges. We did not identify a representative study of adult Frenchton weights; ask about the actual parents and avoid any guaranteed puppy size. For temperament, see our guide to French Bulldog temperament; with a cross, expect a mix rather than a fixed personality.

Frenchton health issues

Here is what the parent breeds bring to a Frenchton.

  • Breathing problems (BOAS). Both breeds have short skulls. In a 2024 UK study, 39 of 104 assessed Boston Terriers (37.5%) received BOAS grade 0, versus 10% of French Bulldogs in an earlier, comparable sample. These are study-group results, not predictions for mixed-breed puppies.[3] That is good news for Bostons, but it does not tell you how a mixed puppy will breathe. Learn what to listen for in our guide to noisy breathing in French Bulldogs.
  • Screw tail and spine malformations. A DVL2 variant linked to screw tails was homozygous in all 79 French Bulldogs genotyped in a 2018 study; Boston Terriers in that study had a high allele frequency (0.94). These sample findings do not establish the genotype or health outcome of every Frenchton. The variant was also associated with some vertebral malformations, with incomplete and variable penetrance.[2] Read more in French Bulldog tail and spine problems.
  • Eye injuries. Flat-faced dogs have prominent eyes. In a UK study, brachycephalic breeds had about 11 times the odds of corneal ulcers compared with crossbreds.[7]
  • Juvenile cataracts. The UC Davis HSF4 DNA test for juvenile hereditary cataracts covers both the French Bulldog and the Boston Terrier. The condition is recessive, so a puppy is affected only if it inherits the variant from both parents.[8]

Heat is a shared risk too: treat a Frenchton like any flat-faced dog on hot days.

Is a Frenchton healthier than a French Bulldog?

Many listings say crosses are healthier thanks to "hybrid vigor." The evidence does not support assuming that. In a 2024 UK study of Cockapoos, Labradoodles and Cavapoos, the odds did not differ significantly between the crosses and their parent breeds in 86.6% of the health comparisons. Where there was a difference, it went both ways: higher risk in 7.0% and lower risk in 6.4%.[4]

Frenchtons were not part of that study. But the logic matters even more here: crossing two flat-faced breeds does not guarantee that their offspring will be free of airway, eye or spinal risks. Breathing ability cannot be predicted from the Frenchton label or the Boston parent's appearance alone. Ask for evidence of functional airway assessment of both breeding dogs and have the puppy examined.

Frenchton lifespan: what is not known

We did not identify a representative, peer-reviewed Frenchton-specific life-expectancy estimate in this audit. Generic lifespan ranges on seller websites should not be treated as evidence. What exists is data on the parents. In a 2024 UK study of more than 584,000 dogs, French Bulldogs had a median lifespan of 9.8 years, among the shortest of all breeds, and flat-faced breeds as a group lived shorter lives than dogs with medium-length faces.[9] For details and caveats, see our guide to French Bulldog lifespan.

Also unknown: whether Frenchtons have fewer spine or breathing problems in practice, and how health changes in second-generation crosses.

Before you buy a Frenchton puppy

Because Frenchtons have no AKC breed standard, the relevant health screenings should be assessed for each parent breed and the individual breeding dogs. Ask the breeder to show you, for both parents:

  • A breathing assessment, such as the Respiratory Function Grading Scheme, recommended by the French Bull Dog Club of America.[10]
  • A recent eye exam by a board-certified veterinary ophthalmologist (CAER) and the HSF4 (JHC) DNA result.[8][10]
  • Hips, kneecaps and heart screening.[10]
  • Open nostrils, quiet breathing at rest and a dog that can walk and play without struggling.

Be wary of "Frenchton merle" puppies sold at a premium. UC Davis notes that merle dogs "may possess a wide range of auditory and ophthalmologic defects."[11] See our guide to merle French Bulldog health risks. Our checklist of breeder health tests explains what each result means.
