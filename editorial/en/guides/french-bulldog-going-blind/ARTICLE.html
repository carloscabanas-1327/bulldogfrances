Is your French Bulldog going blind, or is it just a red or cloudy eye? French Bulldogs do not go blind as a rule, but their prominent eyes are prone to injuries and a few inherited problems that can cost vision if they are missed. This guide separates common myths from facts, explains which Frenchie eye problems really threaten sight, and lists the early signs to watch.

Key takeaways

  • French Bulldogs had about 4.4 times the odds of corneal ulcers compared with other dogs in a large UK study. Deep ulcers can lead to blindness.[1][2]
  • An inherited juvenile cataract can blind young Frenchies by about 2 to 3 years of age; a DNA test identifies carriers.[3]
  • Cherry eye looks alarming, but it is a tear gland that has slipped out of place, not a loss of vision.[4]
  • Most blind dogs with comfortable eyes adapt well, according to veterinary ophthalmologists.[5]

Do French Bulldogs go blind?

Most do not. But eye trouble is common in the breed. In a UK study of French Bulldogs seen by vets in 2013, 10.5% had an eye disorder recorded that year, most often conjunctivitis, cherry eye and corneal ulcers.[6] A later study found French Bulldogs had about 1.5 times the odds of eye disorders overall compared with other dogs.[1]

Most of those problems are treatable. The risk to vision comes when a painful eye is left too long, or when an inherited condition is not caught early.

French Bulldog blindness: myths vs facts

MythFact
"All Frenchies go blind eventually."We found no study showing this. Eye disorders are common in the breed, and the ones recorded most often are conditions such as conjunctivitis, cherry eye and corneal ulcers.[1][6]
"Cherry eye makes them blind."Cherry eye is a tear gland that pops out in the corner of the eye. It is not blindness. Vets prefer to put the gland back, because removing it makes dry eye more likely later.[4]
"A cloudy eye in an older dog is always a cataract."Normal age-related hardening of the lens (nuclear sclerosis) can look similar. Only an exam tells them apart.[7]
"A clear DNA test means no cataracts."The HSF4 test covers only that inherited form.[3] Cataracts also have other causes, such as diabetes, inflammation and injury.[7]
"Bumping into furniture is the first sign."VCA notes it may not appear until blindness is complete. Earlier signs are subtler.[8]
"Blue eyes mean a Frenchie can't see."Eye color alone does not tell you about vision. The concern is the merle gene, which UC Davis says can bring "a wide range of auditory and ophthalmologic defects."[9] See blue-eyed French Bulldogs.

French Bulldog eye problems that can affect vision

Corneal ulcers

The cornea is the clear front of the eye. Flat-faced breeds are more prone to ulcers because of their facial anatomy, and dry eye, rolled-in eyelids and stray lashes add to the risk. Superficial ulcers usually heal in days, but deep ulcers can perforate the eye and cause blindness.[2] Squinting, rubbing, holding an eye shut or a cloudy spot means a same-day vet visit. Our guide to red eyes in French Bulldogs covers the first steps.

Juvenile hereditary cataracts

This inherited cataract, tested through the HSF4 gene, affects the French Bulldog, Boston Terrier and Staffordshire Bull Terrier. Cataracts begin before 1 year of age and progress to blindness by about 2 to 3 years. It is recessive: a puppy must inherit two copies of the mutation to be affected, so two carriers can produce affected puppies.[3] The French Bull Dog Club of America says French Bulldogs should be tested for juvenile hereditary cataracts, and its CHIC health certification requires an eye exam by a board-certified veterinary ophthalmologist.[10]

Dry eye (KCS)

When the eyes make too few tears, the surface becomes inflamed, with thick discharge, redness and a dull look. Cornell lists the English Bulldog and Pug among predisposed breeds and warns that untreated dry eye can scar the cornea, "potentially resulting in permanent vision loss." It usually needs lifelong treatment.[11]

Eye popping out (proptosis)

In flat-faced breeds a blow or a fight can push the eye out of its socket (proptosis). Merck notes brachycephalic breeds are predisposed and that only about 25% of dogs recover vision.[12] This is an emergency: go to a vet immediately, and do not try to push the eye back yourself. Using a harness instead of a collar also avoids extra pressure on the eyes.[13]

French Bulldog going blind: early signs

Vision loss is often gradual, and dogs compensate well, so early signs are easy to miss. VCA lists:[8]

  • Clinginess or anxiety in new places
  • Hesitating on stairs or before jumping on or off furniture
  • Slower walks and less play
  • Startling easily when approached
  • No longer making eye contact

A simple home check: silently drop a cotton ball in front of your dog and see whether its eyes follow it.[8] Also look at the eyes themselves. A white or bluish haze, a cloudy spot or a change in pupil size is worth showing your vet.

French Bulldog sudden blindness and blind in one eye

Sudden blindness is an emergency. Merck groups the causes into three: something blocking light inside the eye (such as severe corneal swelling or a cataract), retinal disease, or problems with the optic nerve or brain.[14] Merck lists sudden vision loss among eye emergencies, so see a vet the same day.

If your Frenchie seems blind in one eye, have that eye examined, and don't wait if it is painful, red or cloudy. For causes that are not breed-specific, such as retinal disease, see our full guide to blindness in dogs.

Living with a blind Frenchie

The American College of Veterinary Ophthalmologists says that if a blind pet's eyes are not painful, most adjust well and lead a fairly normal life using smell and hearing, and that "blindness is often harder on the pet owner than it is on the animal."[5] Their tips:

  • Keep furniture in the same place and set up a fixed spot for food, water and bed.
  • Gate off stairs and drops, and keep your dog away from pools.
  • Use toys that make noise or have a scent, and different floor textures as landmarks.
  • Teach cues such as "stop" and "step up," and speak before you touch your dog.
  • Keep checking blind eyes for squinting, rubbing or discharge.
