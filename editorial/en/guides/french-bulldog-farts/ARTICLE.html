French Bulldog farts can reflect swallowed air, diet changes or digestive illness. New gas with vomiting, weight loss or abdominal pain needs veterinary attention.

Key takeaways

  • VCA vets name diet changes and eating spoiled or unusual food as the most common cause of flatulence in dogs.[1]
  • Some short-nosed dogs may swallow air while eating or breathing; the amount varies by individual and should not be inferred from face shape alone.[1]
  • Selected airway-referral studies describe associated regurgitation or vomiting, not how often the whole French Bulldog population experiences flatulence.[2][3]
  • A swollen, hard belly with repeated unproductive retching is an emergency: go to a vet now.[4]

Why do French Bulldogs fart so much?

VCA defines flatulence as excess gas formed in the stomach or intestines. Its list of causes reads like a description of the typical Frenchie:[1]

  • Swallowed air (aerophagia). Dogs that eat fast gulp air, and brachycephalic breeds "such as bulldogs and pugs" swallow more because they struggle to breathe through their noses.
  • Diet changes and scavenging. Sudden food changes or fatty and spoiled scraps are common triggers, but not necessarily the cause for an individual dog.
  • Inactivity and extra weight. VCA lists obesity and a sedentary lifestyle as contributors.
  • Gut bacteria changes after illness, new foods or medication.

Among 73 brachycephalic dogs referred for airway disease, researchers reported gastrointestinal signs including regurgitation and vomiting.[2][3] This selected group cannot establish the rate of flatulence in otherwise healthy French Bulldogs. Report persistent digestive symptoms and breathing noise together to your vet; see French Bulldog noisy breathing.

Why French Bulldog farts smell so bad

Strong odor generally comes from gut bacteria fermenting food that was not fully digested earlier in the gut. VCA's list of gas triggers fits that picture: high-fat diets, fermentable fibers, legumes and dairy in a dog that cannot digest lactose well.[1]

A sudden change in how bad the gas smells, especially with soft or greasy stool, can mean your dog is not digesting food properly. That is worth a vet visit rather than another food switch.

French Bulldog gas remedies that actually help

Dietary changes should target the likely cause. VCA discusses highly digestible diets and fermentable ingredients, but reducing all fiber or fat or adding probiotics is not appropriate for every dog.[1] Ask your vet when symptoms persist.

  1. Slow down meals. Split the daily ration into more meals and use a slow-feeder bowl or puzzle feeder.
  2. Change food gradually. Mix the new food in over several days instead of switching overnight.
  3. Stop the extras. No dairy, no fatty scraps, no garbage access.[1] AAHA advises keeping treats to no more than 10% of daily calories.[5]
  4. Keep your dog moving with regular walks, adjusted for heat and breathing.
  5. Keep a lean body condition. Extra weight is on VCA's list of contributors.[1]
  6. Ask before supplements. Do not give human gas medicines or probiotics without checking with your vet.

Keep a simple gas diary. For two weeks, note what your dog ate (including treats, chews and anything stolen), how fast it ate, how much exercise it got and when the gas was worst. Patterns can show up quickly: the trigger may turn out to be one type of chew, a family member's snacks or a new bag of food. Bring the diary to your vet if the gas does not improve; it makes the visit far more useful than "he farts a lot."

Ask your veterinarian about persistent or worsening gas rather than making repeated unsupervised food changes. A structured diet trial may be indicated. Food allergy is one possibility, though in dogs it often shows up as itchy skin; see French Bulldog food allergies.

When gas is a sign of a health problem

VCA notes that when an underlying digestive disease is behind the gas, there are usually other signs too: "loose stools or diarrhea, vomiting, and weight loss." Possible causes your vet may want to rule out include intestinal parasites, inflammatory bowel disease, bacterial overgrowth, poor pancreatic function (EPI) and, rarely, tumors. Tests can include stool checks, blood tests and imaging.[1]

Book a vet visit if gas comes with:

  • Diarrhea, mucus or blood in the stool (see French Bulldog diarrhea)
  • Vomiting or regurgitation
  • Weight loss or a dull coat despite a good appetite
  • A clear change from your dog's normal, lasting more than a few days

Emergency signs

Gas in the intestine is not the same as a stomach filling with gas. Bloat (gastric dilatation-volvulus) is "a life-threatening emergency." Merck lists restlessness, discomfort, rapid breathing, abdominal pain and swelling, repeated dry retching and drooling. It mostly affects large, deep-chested breeds, but any dog with these signs needs a vet immediately.[4]

Also go now if your Frenchie has a painful belly, cannot settle, is breathing with effort, or has pale or bluish gums.
