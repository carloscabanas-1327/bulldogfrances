Blindness in dogs can come on over months or almost overnight. Some causes, like acute glaucoma, are emergencies where fast treatment can save the eye; others, like inherited retinal disease, progress slowly, and most dogs adapt better than their owners expect. This guide covers the early signs, the main causes, when sudden blindness is urgent, what can be reversed, and how to set up your home for a blind dog.

Key takeaways

  • Sudden vision loss, or a red, painful eye with a large pupil that doesn't react, is an emergency: acute glaucoma can cause permanent blindness.[1][2]
  • Common causes include cataracts, glaucoma, retinal disease and corneal injury.[3]
  • Diabetic dogs develop cataracts fast: in one study, half had cataracts within 170 days of diagnosis.[4]
  • Most blind dogs whose eyes aren't painful adjust well and live a nearly normal life.[5]

Signs your dog is going blind

VCA describes early signs that are easy to miss:[6]

  • Anxiety or clinginess in new places
  • Hesitating on stairs or before jumping on or off furniture
  • Slower walks and less play
  • Startling easily, sometimes with a defensive snap
  • No longer making eye contact

With progressive retinal atrophy, the first sign is usually poor vision at night or in dim rooms.[7] Bumping into furniture may not show up until blindness is complete, because dogs memorize their home. Some of these signs also overlap with arthritis or canine cognitive decline.[6]

A quick home check: drop a cotton ball in front of your dog and see whether its eyes follow it. Cotton makes no sound, so your dog can't track it by hearing.[6] To check one eye, gently cover the other and repeat. This is a clue, not a diagnosis; your vet will do the real exam.

CataractsThe lens turns cloudy. Often inherited; also from diabetes, inflammation or injury.[8]Usually gradual; can be fast in diabetic dogs.[4]

Sudden blindness in dogs: when it's urgent

Go to the vet immediately if you see eye pain, redness, a dilated pupil that doesn't shrink in light, a hazy cornea or an eye that feels firm. The Merck Veterinary Manual says any dog with signs of glaucoma should be seen right away, because it is a common cause of irreversible blindness.[1][2]

Sudden blindness without pain is urgent too. Acute (sudden) blindness in dogs can come from retinal detachment, SARDS, optic nerve disease or toxicity, and your vet needs to sort these out quickly. If the retina looks normal, referral to a veterinary ophthalmologist is usually the next step.[3]

Using a harness instead of a collar is a smart habit for any dog with eye disease: in one study, collar pressure on the neck raised pressure inside the eye by about 52%, while a harness caused no significant change.[12]

Blindness in dogs with diabetes and in old age

Diabetes: diabetic cataracts can form quickly. In a study of 200 diabetic dogs, 50% had cataracts within 170 days of diagnosis, 75% within 370 days and 80% within 470 days.[4] If your dog is diagnosed with diabetes, ask your vet about regular eye checks and whether a referral to an ophthalmologist makes sense early.

Old age: not every cloudy eye in a senior dog is a cataract. With age, the center of the lens hardens and takes on a bluish-gray haze (nuclear sclerosis), which usually doesn't affect vision much.[8] Older dogs are, however, the usual age group for SARDS, and late-onset PRA typically appears around 3 to 9 years.[7][9] More on aging eyes and bodies in our senior French Bulldog care guide.

Treatment: can blindness in dogs be reversed?

It depends on the cause:

  • Cataracts: surgery can restore vision, and it works best before the cataract is fully mature. Untreated cataracts still need monitoring, because complications such as glaucoma can develop.[8]
  • Acute glaucoma: vision can sometimes be saved with emergency treatment; delay makes permanent loss more likely.[2]
  • PRA and SARDS: no effective treatment has been reported, and antioxidant supplements have shown no measurable benefit for PRA.[7][9]
  • Deep corneal ulcers: need prompt treatment to save the eye.[10]

Living with a blind dog

The American College of Veterinary Ophthalmologists (ACVO) says that if the eyes aren't painful, most pets adapt well using smell and hearing, some within weeks and some almost at once, and that "blindness is often harder on owners than on the pets."[5] Its tips:[5]

  • Keep furniture where it is and set up a fixed "home base" with water, food and bed.
  • Gate off stairs and drops, and keep your dog away from pools.
  • Use noisy or scented toys, and rugs with different textures as landmarks.
  • Put a bell on your other pets.
  • Speak before you touch, and teach cues like "stop" and "step."
  • Use a leash in unfamiliar places.
  • Keep checking the eyes: a blind eye can still hurt or get infected.
