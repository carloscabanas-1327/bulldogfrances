A blue eyed French Bulldog may have pigment variants, but eye color alone cannot establish genetic disease or hearing loss. The merle pattern and extensive white spotting can be associated with eye and hearing concerns, so DNA results and veterinary examinations matter.[1] Here is what causes French Bulldog blue eyes, when a puppy's blue eyes are just temporary, and what to check.

Key takeaways

  • UC Davis notes that blue or partially blue eyes are commonly associated with merle, but coat and eye appearance alone cannot determine a dog's specific merle genotype.[1]
  • Pigment-related deafness often comes with blue eyes,[2] and blue-eyed dogs are statistically more likely to be deaf.[3]
  • All three major breed standards call for dark eyes. The FCI disqualifies eyes of two different colors.[4][5][6]
  • An adult's eye that suddenly looks blue, cloudy or hazy is not a color change. See a vet the same day.[7]

Do French Bulldog eyes stay blue?

Usually not. Many puppies' eyes look blue or blue-gray when they first open, because the iris has not yet built up its final pigment. As the pigment develops over the first weeks and months, most French Bulldog puppies' eyes change color to brown. Ask your vet if you are unsure at a checkup.

So a young puppy with blue eyes tells you little. A Frenchie whose eyes are still clearly blue as an adult is different: the blue is then usually genetic, and it is worth knowing which gene is behind it.

What causes blue eyes in an adult French Bulldog

Blue iris appearance commonly results from reduced pigmentation and light scattering.[3] Several genetic mechanisms can produce blue eyes in dogs, including:[8]

  • Merle (M locus). Merle dilutes pigment in random patches of the coat and often the eyes. UC Davis lists the French Bulldog among the breeds appropriate for merle testing.[1]
  • Piebald and extensive white. White-spotting genes can also leave the iris without pigment.[8]
  • An ALX4 duplication. A 2018 study of more than 3,000 dogs found a DNA duplication near the ALX4 gene strongly linked to blue eyes, mostly in Siberian Huskies and also in non-merle Australian Shepherds. The authors note that before this, merle and piebald were the only known genetic causes.[8] It has not been described as a cause in French Bulldogs.

A gray or "blue" coat is a separate thing. The blue coat color comes from the recessive dilute gene, which turns black pigment gray-blue.[9] A "grey blue eyed French Bulldog" may carry dilute and merle together, so ask for DNA results for both.

Blue eyed merle and white French Bulldogs

Merle-related blue eyes cannot be identified reliably from appearance or a seller's description alone. A dog may inherit one or two merle variants, with different lengths and patterns; some dogs carry cryptic merle. When both parents carry a merle variant, puppies may inherit two (double merle), and UC Davis warns that double merles "may have auditory, ophthalmologic, skeletal, and other defects," which is why breeding two merles is discouraged.[1] Our double merle French Bulldog guide explains the risks.

A white, blue-eyed French Bulldog deserves a careful assessment of hearing and merle/white-spotting genetics, but neither coat color nor eye color alone proves deafness. A mostly white dog from two merle parents may carry two merle variants. See white French Bulldog deafness and our guide to the pied French Bulldog.

French Bulldog blue eyes: health risks

Blue eyes do not hurt and do not, by themselves, mean a dog sees badly. The concern is what often travels with them:

  • Deafness. Merck says congenital deafness "is often associated with blue eyes and white pigmentation" and is most common in dogs with piebald or merle genes.[2] In merle dogs studied by Strain and colleagues, deafness was uncommon with one copy of merle and much more common with two.[10] A BAER test checks each ear.
  • Eye defects in double merles. These can be serious and present from birth.[1]
  • Iris colobomas. These are gaps in the iris. Merck notes they are "most common in eyes that have more than one color in the iris."[11] Many cause no problems, but an eye vet should look.
  • Squinting is not a "blue eye" trait. If a blue-eyed Frenchie squints, or has redness or discharge, treat it as an eye problem; see French Bulldog red eyes.

Blue eyes and the breed standard

  • AKC (US): eyes "dark, brown or approaching black in color"; lighter brown eyes are "acceptable, but not desirable", and blue or green eyes, or any trace of blue or green, are a disqualification.[4]
  • FCI: eyes dark; "light eyes" are a severe fault and eyes of two different colors (heterochromia) are disqualifying.[5]
  • Royal Kennel Club (UK): eyes "preferably dark and matching."[6] Merle Frenchies are not registered because merle "carries an increased risk of impaired hearing and sight problems."[12]

None of the standards treats blue eyes as a desirable trait.

When "blue" means an eye problem

If an adult French Bulldog's eye develops new blue-gray cloudiness, do not assume the iris has changed color. Corneal edema, lens changes or elevated eye pressure are among the possible explanations; only an eye examination can distinguish them. Merck lists swelling and discoloration of the cornea, redness and an enlarged eye among glaucoma signs and says the dog "should be seen by a veterinarian immediately."[7]

Seek emergency veterinary care immediately for a painful, red and cloudy eye, sudden vision loss or a suddenly bulging or displaced globe. A slow, painless haze in an older dog also needs an exam; read signs a French Bulldog is going blind.

Buying a blue eyed French Bulldog puppy

Searches for a blue eyed French Bulldog price or puppies for sale often lead to merle litters marketed by eye color. If you are still considering one, ask the seller for:

  1. M-locus (merle) DNA results for both parents and the puppy.
  2. A BAER hearing test result for each ear.
  3. An eye exam by a veterinary ophthalmologist.
  4. The parents' other health tests; see our breeder health tests guide.

Avoid any litter from two merle parents.
