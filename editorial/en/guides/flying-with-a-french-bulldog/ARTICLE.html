Flying with a French Bulldog requires checking airline restrictions, carrier fit and your dog's airway health before booking.

Key takeaways

  • As of November 2026, American and Delta restrict snub-nosed dog breeds in checked or cargo travel. Confirm current eligibility with each airline, including Alaska/Hawaiian.[1][2][3]
  • Cabin eligibility depends on the airline and aircraft: the carrier must fit under the seat and provide adequate space for the dog. Do not assume a universal size or weight allowance.[1][4]
  • Ask your veterinarian well before travel whether your Frenchie is healthy enough to fly; do not give sedatives unless the veterinarian has reviewed airline restrictions and risks.[5]
  • Airline embargoes and pet acceptance can change after publication. Confirm the exact flight, aircraft, route and current pet policy before buying tickets.[6][7]

Can French Bulldogs fly in cargo?

Often restricted, particularly on large US airlines, because short-nosed breeds face greater breathing and heat risks. Some affected dogs have narrow nostrils, elongated soft palates or other airway abnormalities; anatomy and disease severity vary. Heat or stress may worsen obstruction and impair cooling. An owner cannot monitor and assist a pet in the cargo compartment during the flight.[5] The USDA warns that short-nosed dogs face a higher risk of heatstroke and breathing problems when exposed to stress or extreme heat.[8]

Historical US Department of Transportation data found disproportionate short-faced dog deaths during an earlier five-year period. Those old event counts are not a current mortality rate or the risk for an individual flight.[5] And a UK study of heatstroke in dogs found the French Bulldog among the breeds at significantly higher risk.[9] Larger US airlines must report every animal death, injury or loss in transport to the DOT.[10]

Flying with a French Bulldog in the cabin

For most Frenchies, the cabin is the only realistic option. Three things decide whether yours can go:

  • Size: American's soft-sided limit is 18 × 11 × 11 inches, and your dog must stay in the carrier under the seat in front of you for the whole flight.[1] The AKC standard allows French Bulldogs up to 28 lb, so many adults are a tight fit.[12] Your dog should be able to stand and turn around; a squashed, overheated Frenchie is at real risk.
  • Age and entry rules: eligible domestic pets may be carried by some airlines from 8 weeks old, but all dogs entering or returning to the United States must be at least 6 months old under current CDC rules.[1][18]
  • Space on the flight: cabin pet spots can be limited, so add your dog to the booking when you buy your ticket.

Plan your luggage around the carrier: American lets you bring only one additional item on board with your pet.[1]

Flying internationally

Many international airlines set a cabin weight limit instead. As of November 2026, Lufthansa, KLM and Iberia all cap cabin pets at 8 kg (about 17.6 lb) including the carrier, and all three list bulldogs among the snub-nosed breeds they will not carry in the hold as checked pets. Each points owners to a separate cargo or air-freight service instead, which carries the same heat and breathing risks.[13][14][15][16] Since an adult French Bulldog typically weighs 18 to 31 lb (8 to 14 kg) under the FCI standard, most adults will not qualify.[17]

For entry or re-entry to the United States, the CDC requires dogs to appear healthy, be at least 6 months old, have a readable microchip and a CDC Dog Import Form receipt. Additional documents and permissible entry ports depend on countries visited in the prior 6 months and where rabies vaccination occurred. Foreign-vaccinated dogs from high-risk countries face additional facility requirements.[18] Use the CDC Dog Importation Navigator for your exact itinerary before booking.

Tips for flying with a French Bulldog

  1. Ask your vet about fitness to fly in advance, and ask honestly whether your dog is fit to fly.[5] A Frenchie that snores loudly or gasps after mild exercise may not be; read our guide to French Bulldog noisy breathing.
  2. Keep a healthy weight. Extra pounds make breathing harder for short-nosed dogs.[5] See overweight French Bulldog.
  3. Get your dog used to the carrier weeks ahead, with treats and short sessions.[5]
  4. Do not self-medicate or sedate. Sedatives can affect breathing and temperature control. Have your veterinarian assess any medication and confirm airline permission.[5]
  5. Avoid heat. Pick nonstop flights at cooler times of day. For checked and cargo pets, American says pets cannot travel when the current or forecast temperature is above 85°F anywhere on the itinerary, and it also restricts travel below 45°F.[1] Alaska may refuse pets when the forecast is above 85°F or below 45°F.[3]

Heat on the ground is just as dangerous: never leave your dog in a parked car, even for a few minutes. More in our guide to French Bulldog heat safety.

If your Frenchie is too big for the cabin

If your dog will not fit comfortably under the seat, do not assume a cargo workaround is acceptable or safe. Discuss alternatives with your veterinarian, including postponement, a carefully managed road trip or care at home. If your dog is staying behind, our guide on whether French Bulldogs can be left alone will help you plan.
