Oklahoma City's municipal animal shelter has stopped taking in and adopting out dogs after canine distemper turned up inside the building.[1][2] The outbreak reported so far is inside the shelter, not across the city. Still, it is a good reminder for every Frenchie household, in Oklahoma or anywhere else, to check one line on the vaccine record before the holiday boarding and daycare season. Here is what happened, what the virus does, and what to look at this week.

Key takeaways

  • On November 19, 2026, Oklahoma City Animal Welfare suspended dog adoptions and dog intakes after five shelter dogs tested positive for canine distemper.[1][2]

What happened at the Oklahoma City shelter

Oklahoma City Animal Welfare announced on Thursday, November 19, 2026, that five dogs in its care had tested positive for canine distemper and that three had been euthanized because of symptoms.[1][2] The shelter holds about 200 dogs.[1]

Dog adoptions and routine dog intakes are on hold for at least two weeks. According to KOCO, they will restart once the virus is no longer present in the shelter population, and in the meantime the shelter only accepts dogs that are an immediate threat to the public or need emergency care.[1] Dogs that were exposed or show symptoms are being isolated, and staff are cleaning and disinfecting the building.[2] Owners can still reclaim a lost pet there.[2]

Cats and other animals remain available for adoption. The shelter says domestic cats are not susceptible to canine distemper.[1] (Veterinary references do list large wild cats among the species the virus can infect, so that statement is about pet cats only.[3])

"Canine distemper is a serious disease. Vaccination, quarantining and limiting exposure is our best defense."

— Johny Sandoval, shelter superintendent, in the shelter's news release[1][2]

Sandoval also said the shelter is exploring low-cost vaccination clinics for Oklahoma City residents with community partners, and asked owners to keep pets fully vaccinated, wearing an ID tag and properly contained.[1]

What canine distemper is

Canine distemper is a viral disease that can attack the respiratory, digestive and nervous systems.[1] It passes between animals mainly through droplets in the respiratory secretions of infected animals, and some infected dogs keep shedding the virus for several months.[3] Domestic dogs are considered the main reservoir of the virus.[3]

The good news is in the same reference: vaccination is available and generally effective, and widespread vaccination of pet dogs is described as essential to control the disease.[3] The virus is also fragile outside the body and is killed by most common disinfectants, which is why cleaning protocols matter in kennels and clinics.[3] For a longer explainer, read our guide to distemper in dogs.

Signs to watch for

The shelter asks anyone who adopted a dog from it in the past three weeks to watch for:[1][2]

  • signs of respiratory illness and nasal discharge;
  • fever, low energy (lethargy) and loss of appetite;
  • diarrhea;
  • neurological signs such as a head tilt, walking in circles or unusual eye movements;
  • seizures.

According to the Merck Veterinary Manual, a first fever often appears 3 to 6 days after infection and can go unnoticed. Discharge from the nose and eyes, tiredness and poor appetite may follow, and nervous-system signs such as twitching or seizures can show up weeks or even months later.[3]

In a French Bulldog, a runny nose and noisy breathing are easy to dismiss as "just the breed." Do not wait it out if they come with fever, sticky eye discharge or a dog that will not eat: call your veterinarian, and mention any recent shelter, boarding or daycare contact so the clinic can take precautions before you walk in.

What Frenchie owners should check now

Nothing in the reports suggests French Bulldogs are at higher risk than other dogs. The practical steps are the same for every breed:

  • Look at the vaccine record. Find the date of your dog's last distemper vaccination and ask your vet whether it is current. Your vet will decide the schedule; we do not give one here.
  • Puppies first. A puppy that has not finished its vaccine series is the most vulnerable. Our French Bulldog puppy care guide explains why the full series matters.
  • Before boarding or daycare. Ask whether the facility requires proof of vaccination for every dog and how it isolates a dog that gets sick. Holiday travel often means a few nights away from home; a kennel stay can also expose dogs to kennel cough.
  • Dog parks and new dogs. Keep an unvaccinated puppy, or a dog whose vaccine status is unclear, away from places where many unknown dogs mix until your vet clears it.
  • If you live near Oklahoma City. Follow the shelter's updates before you plan to adopt or surrender a dog, and keep an ID tag on your Frenchie in case it gets lost.[1]

What we do not know yet

The reports do not say how the virus entered the shelter, and there is no sign so far of an outbreak outside it.[1][2] There is no exact date for dog adoptions to resume: the shelter has only said "at least two weeks."[1] We will not update this article with new case counts unless the shelter publishes them.
