Which documents are essential to review?

Ask for a signed purchase or adoption agreement, vaccination and veterinary records, any parasite-prevention history, identification details, and the name of the clinic that examined the puppy.

The exact legally required documents depend on where the transaction occurs. A checklist of good practice should never be mistaken for a uniform legal requirement across the United States and Canada.

Should you receive pedigree papers?

A registration certificate or transfer paperwork can document pedigree or registry status when applicable. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that the puppy is free of inherited disease.

Verify the issuing organization and any conditions on registration. Ask what happens if the registration transfer has not been completed by the time the puppy goes home.

What about the parents' medical records?

Request independent health-testing records for both parents, with enough identifying information to match the results to the correct dogs. For French Bulldogs, inquire about hip, patella, cardiac and eye evaluation, plus airway assessments where available.

A brochure or social-media claim is not the same as a dated laboratory result or clinical evaluation.

What should a contract cover?

Read the identity of the parties, the individual puppy's identification, what is included at transfer, the agreed procedure if illness is identified, and any return or rehoming provisions.

Avoid signing a contract under pressure. Legal protections and required disclosures can vary by state, province, and transaction type; seek local advice if a clause is unclear.

What should you do after taking the puppy home?

Arrange a veterinary visit promptly, bring every record, and ask your vet to review nutrition, vaccination planning, breathing, ears, eyes, and skin.

Keep original paperwork and digital copies. If details conflict, request written clarification from the seller or rescue organization.
