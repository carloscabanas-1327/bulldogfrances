A dry French Bulldog nose alone cannot diagnose illness. Crusts, bleeding or painful cracks need veterinary assessment to rule out other causes.

Key takeaways

  • In our view, how the nose feels tells you less about your dog's health than its appetite, energy, drinking, breathing and bathroom habits.
  • Nasal hyperkeratosis involves excess keratin on the nose. A 2025 treatment pilot included 20 dogs already diagnosed with the condition, 12 of them French Bulldogs; this does not estimate the breed's prevalence.[1]
  • A 20-dog open-label study reported improved scores after 60 days of a specific balm. With no control group or blinding and funding by the balm's manufacturer (which also employed several authors), efficacy and safety remain preliminary.[1]
  • Sores, bleeding, loss of pigment or changes in the nose's texture need a vet to rule out other conditions.

Is a dry nose a sign of illness?

Not by itself, in our view. We found no study that links a dry nose alone to illness. Judge your dog's health by appetite, energy, drinking, breathing and bathroom habits, not by how the nose feels.

Flat-faced breeds may also be more prone to a thick, crusty nose. The authors of the study below note that abnormal anatomy and keratin build-up may predispose brachycephalic breeds to nasal hyperkeratosis, although little has been published on the subject.[1]

Nasal hyperkeratosis in French Bulldogs

Hyperkeratosis means the skin makes too much keratin, the tough protein in the outer layer of skin. On the nose, it shows up as:

  • Thick, rough, crusty skin, often on the top edge of the nose
  • Hard, feathery or horn-like growths
  • Dry cracks that can become sore or bleed
  • Sometimes similar thickening on the paw pads

French Bulldogs accounted for 12 of 20 animals recruited with nasal hyperkeratosis in a 2025 product trial. This selected clinical sample cannot establish population prevalence or a French Bulldog-specific risk ratio.[1] A veterinarian should first confirm the cause of nasal crusting.

In the 2025 pilot, mean lesion scores fell 54.5% by investigator assessment and 62.3% by owner assessment at 60 days.[1] The trial had only 20 dogs, no comparator and no blinding, and was manufacturer funded. No adverse events were reported, but the sample cannot establish general safety or prove the balm caused the improvement.

Other causes of a dry or cracked nose

  • Sunburn, especially on pink or light-colored noses
  • Allergies, which are common in French Bulldogs. A UK study found about 7.7 times the odds of allergic skin disorder compared with other dogs.[2]
  • Dehydration, usually together with other signs like lethargy and dry gums
  • Autoimmune skin diseases, which can cause loss of pigment, sores or a smooth, shiny nose surface
  • Infections

Safe home care

  • Ask your vet to recommend a dog-safe nose balm. Many contain shea butter, plant oils or ceramides.
  • Use a veterinarian-approved nose product only as directed; avoid inserting it into the nostrils and stop if irritation occurs.
  • Avoid human products with zinc oxide, tea tree oil, salicylic acid, menthol or fragrance. Dogs lick their noses, so ingredients get swallowed.
  • Do not pick or peel crusts. This can cause bleeding and infection.
  • Discuss very dry indoor air with your veterinarian; a humidifier is not an established treatment for nasal hyperkeratosis.
  • Provide shade and limit midday sun, which also protects your Frenchie from heat.

When to see a vet

Book a visit if you notice:

  • Cracks, sores or bleeding
  • Loss of color on the nose, or a nose that changes from rough to smooth and shiny
  • Crusting that spreads to the lips, eyes or paws
  • Discharge from the nose, sneezing or nosebleeds
  • A dry nose together with lethargy, poor appetite or breathing changes

Take photos over a few weeks. They help your vet see how the nose is changing.
