French Bulldog back pain or sudden weakness in the hind legs can signal a spinal problem, including intervertebral disc disease (IVDD). If your dog cannot stand or is dragging its legs, restrict movement and seek emergency veterinary care now.[1]

Key takeaways

  • Intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) happens when a spinal disc bulges or ruptures and presses on the spinal cord.[1]
  • French Bulldogs had about 3 times the odds of spinal cord disorders compared with other dogs in a large UK study.[2]
  • Sudden weakness, dragging legs or loss of bladder control is an emergency. Cornell warns that delaying surgery can mean the spinal cord does not recover.[1]
  • A DNA test can detect a CDDY variant linked with disc disease risk, but it does not diagnose IVDD or predict if an individual dog will develop signs.[3]

French Bulldog Back Pain: IVDD and Other Causes

Between each pair of vertebrae sits a disc with a jelly-like center. In IVDD, that disc degenerates and can bulge or burst into the spinal canal, pressing on the spinal cord.[1] Cornell notes that about 65% of IVDD cases involve the middle-to-lower back and about 18% involve the neck.[1]

In a UK study involving 2,781 French Bulldogs, the breed had 3.00 times the adjusted odds of a diagnosed spinal cord disorder compared with non-French Bulldogs; this is not a threefold absolute probability for any individual dog.[2] UC Davis describes CDDY as a variant associated with intervertebral disc disease risk, not a definitive diagnosis.[3] The Merck Veterinary Manual also describes congenital vertebral malformations in screw-tailed dogs; not all malformations lead to neurological signs.[4] The AKC lists chondrodysplasia and CDDY-related testing among genetic tests available for French Bulldogs.[5]

Signs of back pain

  • Hunched back or tense belly
  • Crying out when picked up or touched
  • Reluctance to jump, climb stairs or go for walks
  • Shivering or panting without an obvious reason
  • Stiff neck, or avoiding turning the head to one side[1]
  • Wobbly, drunken-looking walk in the back legs[1]
  • Knuckling: the paw flips over so the dog walks on the top of the foot

Emergency signs

Go to an emergency vet immediately if your dog:

  • Cannot stand or use its back legs
  • Drags its legs
  • Loses control of its bladder or bowels
  • Seems to have no feeling in its back paws

Cornell describes severe disc disease as leaving a dog "paralyzed, unable to get up or use their rear legs at all," and possibly incontinent. If surgery is recommended, its advice is: "don't wait. The longer you wait, the greater the chance the spinal cord will degenerate to the point where it might not recover."[1]

How IVDD is treated

Medical management. Cornell says mild cases "may be managed medically, at least initially," with strict rest, pain medication and possibly muscle relaxants.[1] Your veterinarian will determine the appropriate period of restricted activity and what movement is safe. Do not end prescribed rest early or give medication without veterinary guidance.

Surgery. For severe or worsening cases, a surgeon removes bone over the affected disc to take the pressure off the spinal cord. Cornell notes recovery "can take months."[1] A neurologist may recommend MRI or CT imaging to identify the affected disc when specialized imaging is needed.[1]

Rehabilitation. Physical therapy, controlled exercise and weight management help many dogs regain strength after either type of treatment.

Other causes of weak back legs

Not every dog with weak back legs has IVDD. Congenital vertebral malformations, injuries and other neurological or orthopedic conditions can cause similar signs.[4] A veterinarian must distinguish the possibilities through an examination and, when appropriate, imaging.[1]

Protecting your Frenchie's back

  • Keep your dog lean. Extra weight strains the spine.
  • Use ramps or steps for beds and sofas, and block stairs where possible.
  • Pick up your dog with two hands, supporting the chest and the rear together.
  • Use a harness, not a collar, especially for dogs with neck pain.
  • Avoid rough play that involves twisting and jumping.
  • If buying a puppy, ask about spinal disease in related dogs and discuss what any genetic testing can and cannot show.[3]
