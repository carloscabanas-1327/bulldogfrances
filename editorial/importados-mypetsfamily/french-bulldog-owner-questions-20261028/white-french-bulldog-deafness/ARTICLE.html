White French Bulldog coats can have different genetic causes. Some pigmentation variants are associated with congenital deafness, but coat color alone cannot diagnose hearing loss.[1]

Key takeaways

  • The Merck Veterinary Manual says congenital deafness in dogs "is associated with white pigmentation," especially in dogs with piebald or merle genes.[1]
  • White is an accepted AKC coat color, but the cited sources do not provide a representative French Bulldog-specific estimate of normal hearing or deafness in white dogs.[2]
  • Deafness in one ear can go unnoticed. A BAER test provides the most definitive veterinary assessment and tests each ear separately.[1]
  • Some predominantly white dogs carry double-merle genotypes associated with elevated health concerns; white appearance itself does not establish merle genotype.[3]

Why white coats and deafness are linked

Certain inherited white-spotting and merle-associated pigment changes can disrupt melanocyte-dependent inner-ear function. Merck describes stria vascularis degeneration in one form of pigment-associated congenital deafness; not every dog with light or white hair has this mechanism or is deaf.[1]

Merck describes this inherited cochleosaccular form as developing in the first weeks after birth and potentially affecting one or both ears. This specific form is generally not treatable, but dogs can also acquire hearing loss later from unrelated causes such as ear disease, injury or aging.[1]

White, piebald or double merle?

A "white" French Bulldog can come from different genetic backgrounds, and the risk differs:

  • White, cream or very light fawn: accepted colors under the AKC standard.[2] A light fawn or cream coat is not the same as white spotting.
  • Piebald (white with patches): accepted in the AKC standard and among the pigment patterns Merck associates with inherited deafness. The cited source does not quantify risk by French Bulldog head or ear marking.[1][2]
  • Double merle (sometimes mostly white): a dog with merle variants inherited from both parents. UC Davis documents complex allele sizes and health concerns. A study found hearing loss in 25% of its sampled double-merle dogs across several breeds, not a French Bulldog-specific prevalence figure.[3][4]

White or cream-colored pigment can arise through mechanisms other than merle, including dilution of red/yellow pigment; UC Davis documents an intensity-dilution variant in French Bulldogs.[5] If merle is suspected in either parent, request a laboratory merle genotype rather than guessing from coat appearance.[3]

Signs your puppy may not hear

  • Sleeps through loud noises, like a door slam or a vacuum cleaner
  • Does not turn toward its name or a squeaky toy out of sight
  • Startles when touched from behind
  • Barks unusually loudly or plays very roughly with littermates

Hand-clapping tests are not reliable. Puppies can feel vibration and see movement, and a dog with one hearing ear responds to sound normally. Merck notes that unilateral deafness "may go undetected" without proper testing.[1]

The BAER hearing test

BAER (brainstem auditory evoked response) measures the brain's electrical response to clicks played into each ear. Merck calls it the test that "provides the most definitive diagnosis."[1]

  • It tests each ear separately, so it detects one-sided deafness.
  • It is quick and usually done without general anesthesia.
  • It is often done from around 5 to 6 weeks of age, once the ear canals have opened. Ask the testing clinic about their minimum age.
  • Veterinary schools, neurology clinics and some breed-club events offer it.

Merck adds that BAER testing combined with careful breeding choices is often the best way to lower deafness within a breed.[1]

Raising a deaf Frenchie

  • Teach hand signals for the basics. Use a "thumbs up" or open hand as a marker for good behavior.
  • Use gentle, reward-based visual and touch cues. A vibration cue should be introduced carefully with positive reinforcement; avoid shock collars and startle-based training.
  • Always leash outside or use a secure fence.
  • Wake gently with a light touch on the shoulder, so the dog does not startle and snap.
  • Add an ID tag that says "I am deaf."

A dog deaf in one ear usually needs no special training, although it may have trouble telling which direction a sound came from.

Questions to ask a breeder

  • "Has this puppy had a BAER test? Can I see the results for each ear?"
  • "Are either of the parents merle or cryptic merle? Do you have M-locus DNA results?"
  • "Have any puppies from these parents been deaf?"
