French Bulldog breathing problems can show up as snoring, snorting or wheezing. These noises are common, but they can signal blocked airways. Learn which signs call for a veterinary exam and which need emergency care.[1]

French Bulldog breathing problems: causes and signs

French Bulldogs are brachycephalic, meaning their skull and muzzle are short. The soft tissue inside the nose and throat did not shrink as much as the bones did. The result can be narrow nostrils, a long soft palate, and crowded tissue in the throat. Together these make up brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome, or BOAS.[1]

The problem is widespread. A Royal Veterinary College study of 2,781 French Bulldogs under UK veterinary care found they had about 31 times the odds of a BOAS diagnosis and 42 times the odds of narrowed nostrils (stenotic nares) compared with other breeds.[2]

Researchers in Cambridge measured 604 Pugs, French Bulldogs and Bulldogs and found that stenotic nostrils were associated with BOAS in all three breeds. In French Bulldogs, neck measurements were also linked to risk.[3]

Normal or not? The warning signs

Because so many Frenchies snore, owners often assume it is part of the breed. The Cambridge BOAS team lists the signs that point to airway disease:[1]

  • Noise while awake, not just during sleep, especially at rest.
  • Visible effort: the belly working hard, or the lips pulled back while breathing.
  • Poor exercise and heat tolerance: needing to stop and recover after a short walk, or struggling in mild warmth.
  • Sleep problems: sleeping sitting up, with the chin propped on a toy, or waking suddenly as if choking (sleep apnea).
  • Gagging, regurgitation or vomiting, which often occur alongside BOAS.
  • Narrow, slit-like nostrils that pinch closed when the dog breathes in.

If your dog is breathing comfortably, a short video of normal resting breathing or an ordinary gentle activity may help your vet. Never make a dog exercise to reproduce a breathing problem. A video cannot replace a veterinary examination.

When it is an emergency

Go to an emergency vet right away if your dog has any of these signs of severe respiratory distress:[1]

  • Blue, purple or gray gums or tongue (cyanosis)
  • Collapse or fainting
  • Loud, high-pitched noise with frantic effort to breathe
  • Breathing trouble together with heat exposure

Keep the dog calm and as cool as possible on the way. Do not force food, water or exercise.

How vets grade breathing

The University of Cambridge developed a functional grading system based on a three-minute trotting test. A trained assessor listens to the dog's breathing before and after exercise and assigns a grade:[1]

GradeWhat it means
0BOAS free.
IClinically unaffected, but with mild respiratory signs.
IIBOAS affected, with moderate signs. Needs management, which may include weight loss or surgery.
IIIBOAS affected, with severe signs. Needs a thorough veterinary exam and surgery.

This system is the basis of the Respiratory Function Grading Scheme run by the Royal Kennel Club and the University of Cambridge. Dogs can be graded from 12 months of age.[4] In the US, the French Bull Dog Club of America lists this grading as a recommended test for breeding dogs.[5]

What you can do at home

  • Keep your dog at a healthy body condition. Cambridge links obesity with BOAS severity; ask your vet to assess body condition rather than relying only on a breed-average weight.[6]
  • Use a well-fitting harness for walks. Avoid leash pressure on the neck; Blue Cross explains that pulling against a collar can trigger coughing and a harness can help prevent that pressure.[7]
  • Avoid heat and humidity. Choose cooler times of day, keep your dog cool and stop activity if breathing becomes difficult.[6]
  • Match exercise to your dog’s breathing. Avoid strenuous activity or overheating, and ask your veterinarian what activity is appropriate for your dog.[6]
  • Track changes. Note when noise gets louder, when recovery takes longer, or when sleep gets worse, and share it with your vet.

Treatment options, including surgery

For dogs graded II or III, the Cambridge team recommends management and/or treatment, which can include weight reduction and surgery.[1] If anatomical obstruction warrants an operation, procedures may widen the nostrils, shorten an overlong soft palate or address other obstructing tissue.[8]

Ask your veterinarian whether referral to an experienced soft-tissue surgeon is appropriate; the timing depends on the dog's findings and condition. ACVS describes a better prognosis in younger animals and warns that chronic laryngeal changes may limit improvement.[8] Surgery carries anesthesia and postoperative airway risks and cannot remove every effect of brachycephalic conformation, so weight and heat management still matter.[6][8]
