French Bulldog red eyes can signal irritation, a painful corneal ulcer or another urgent eye problem. If your dog suddenly cannot see, has an eye injury or the eyeball appears displaced, seek emergency veterinary care immediately.[5]

Key takeaways

  • In a UK veterinary-record study, French Bulldogs had 4.38 times the adjusted odds of a corneal ulcer diagnosis relative to other dogs (95% CI 2.95–6.50). This is not an individual probability.[1]
  • Squinting, a cloudy cornea or holding the eye closed needs urgent veterinary examination; sudden vision loss or an eye displaced from its socket is an emergency.[3][5]
  • A red lump in the inner corner of the eye is often "cherry eye." Vets usually reposition the gland rather than remove it.[2]
  • Do not use leftover eye medication: steroid-containing drops are unsafe when a corneal ulcer is present.[6]

French Bulldog Red Eyes: Why the Breed Is Vulnerable

A Royal Veterinary College study compared 2,781 French Bulldogs with 21,850 other dogs in UK clinics and found higher adjusted odds of recorded corneal ulceration (aOR 4.38; 95% CI 2.95–6.50). These comparative odds do not predict an individual dog’s outcome, nor do they directly measure US prevalence.[1]

Common causes of red eyes

  • Conjunctivitis: inflamed pink tissue around the eye, often with discharge. Interestingly, the UK study did not find Frenchies more prone to conjunctivitis than other dogs.[1]
  • Corneal ulcer: a scratch or erosion on the clear front of the eye. Merck notes most ulcers in dogs "are caused by injury, such as nail scratches, foreign objects in the eye, or chemicals."[3]
  • Other painful eye disorders: acute glaucoma and lens displacement can be eye emergencies. Redness alone cannot distinguish these conditions from conjunctivitis or a corneal ulcer.[5]
  • Dry eye (low tear production): persistent thick discharge, irritation and redness are possible signs.[2]
  • Cherry eye: a prolapsed gland of the third eyelid can appear as a red mass at the inner eye corner.[2]
  • Facial fold or eyelid contact: physical features can irritate the corneal surface; a veterinarian can assess the lids and folds.[1][3]

Urgent Red-Eye Signs: Same Day or Emergency?

Seek same-day veterinary assessment for a painful, cloudy or squinting eye; suspected acute glaucoma needs emergency care. Go to an emergency clinic immediately for sudden blindness, an eye forced forward or out of the socket, significant eye trauma, or a suspected penetrating injury.[5]

Other signs that need urgent examination include:

  • Squints or keeps one eye closed
  • Paws or rubs at the eye
  • Has a cloudy, white or bluish spot on the eye
  • Has an eye that looks bigger, bulging or out of place (emergency if suddenly displaced)
  • Has a sudden change in vision (emergency if sudden loss of sight)
  • Had an injury, such as a scratch from a cat or a branch

Merck notes that corneal ulcers can become deep or perforate the cornea; the treatment and expected course depend on examination findings. Do not delay diagnosis because an eye looks only mildly red.[3][5]

Cherry eye

Dogs have a third eyelid with a tear gland at its base. In "cherry eye," that gland pops out and appears as a red lump in the inner corner. Merck lists the English Bulldog among the breeds most often affected.[2]

Because the gland makes a large share of the eye's tears, Merck says vets "will want to preserve it, if possible," usually by stitching it back into place. Removing the gland is avoided because dogs with cherry eye "appear to be more likely to develop dry eye ... later in life, especially if the gland is removed."[2] Discuss whether gland preservation and repositioning are possible for your dog; the appropriate procedure depends on the veterinary eye examination.[2]

At home: what to do and not do

Do:

  • Gently wipe discharge away with a clean, damp cotton pad, one per eye.
  • Prevent rubbing. A soft cone may be needed.
  • Take a clear photo to show your vet.

Do not:

  • Use old or human eye drops. Corticosteroids are contraindicated when a corneal ulcer is present; a veterinarian must first check the eye.[6]
  • Wait several days to see if a squinting eye improves.
  • Try to push a cherry eye back in yourself.

Prevention and screening

  • Ask your veterinarian whether facial folds, eyelids or hairs may be rubbing the cornea, and follow any prescribed eye-area care.[3]
  • Avoid letting your dog stick its head out of car windows.
  • Be careful around cats, thorny plants and tall grass.
  • If buying a puppy, ask for the parents' eye-screening records. The French Bull Dog Club of America lists a CAER eye exam among CHIC-required health screens and juvenile hereditary cataract (HSF4-1) testing among additional recommended tests.[4]
