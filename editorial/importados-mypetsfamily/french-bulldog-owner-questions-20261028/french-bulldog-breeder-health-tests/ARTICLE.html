A reputable French Bulldog breeder can show you health results for both parents. Here is exactly what to ask for, and how to read it.

Key takeaways

  • The French Bull Dog Club of America (FBDCA) asks for four screenings before a dog gets a CHIC number: hips, eyes, patellas (kneecaps) and heart.[1]
  • A CHIC number shows the tests were done and published. It does not mean every result was normal.[2]
  • Breathing is the breed's biggest health problem, so ask whether the parents were graded for BOAS (the airway disease of flat-faced dogs).[3]
  • You can check most results yourself for free in the OFA online database.

Why health testing matters so much in this breed

The French Bulldog has been the most popular breed in the American Kennel Club's rankings four years in a row, from 2022 through 2025.[4] Popularity attracts people who breed for profit rather than health. The result shows up in veterinary records. A large study by the Royal Veterinary College compared 2,781 French Bulldogs with 21,850 other dogs in UK veterinary clinics. French Bulldogs had significantly higher odds for 20 of 43 common disorders.[5]

Some of those differences were large. Compared with other dogs, French Bulldogs had about 31 times the odds of being diagnosed with brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS) and about 42 times the odds of narrowed nostrils.[5] The authors concluded the breed "should, in many respects, no longer be considered as an 'average' or 'typical' dog."

You cannot test away every risk. But parents with documented, good results give a puppy a much better start than parents with no records at all.

The four tests the breed club asks for

The FBDCA is the AKC parent club for the breed. Through the Canine Health Information Center (CHIC), it requires that a dog be entered in four registries before it receives a CHIC number:[1]

  • Hips: an OFA hip X-ray evaluation or a PennHIP evaluation, with results registered with the Orthopedic Foundation for Animals (OFA).
  • Eyes: a CAER eye exam performed by a board-certified veterinary ophthalmologist (DACVO). The club recommends repeating it every year.
  • Patellas: a veterinary check for patellar luxation, a kneecap that slips out of place.
  • Heart: a basic cardiac exam by listening with a stethoscope, or an advanced exam using an echocardiogram (ultrasound of the heart).

The dog must also be permanently identified with a microchip or tattoo, so the results can be tied to that specific animal.[1] The American Kennel Club lists the same four evaluations as the recommended health tests for French Bulldog breeding stock, noting that an echocardiogram is "recommended but not required."[6]

Recommended extras: breathing, cataracts and more

Beyond the four required tests, the FBDCA lists several recommended ones:[1]

  • Respiratory Function Grading Scheme (RFGS): a structured breathing assessment developed by the University of Cambridge and the UK Kennel Club. This is the single most useful extra for this breed.
  • Juvenile hereditary cataracts (HSF4): a DNA test. The club says it is important to order the specific HSF4 test for French Bulldogs.
  • Cystinuria type 3: a DNA test linked to bladder stones. The club strongly recommends testing through PennGen.
  • Tracheal hypoplasia (a narrow windpipe) and elbow dysplasia.

The breathing grade deserves extra attention. In the Cambridge scheme, a dog is assessed before and after a 3-minute trotting exercise test. Dogs are graded from 0 to III. Grades 0 and I are considered clinically unaffected, while grades II and III are considered BOAS-affected and need management or surgery.[3] Dogs can be graded from 12 months of age, and breeding dogs should be re-graded every two years.[7]

A word on DNA panels: many breeders advertise a "full DNA panel" that mostly covers coat colors. Color results are not health screening. A puppy's color tells you nothing about its hips, heart or breathing.

What a CHIC number does and does not prove

A CHIC number confirms that the required tests were performed and the results were made public. OFA states plainly that it does not imply the results were normal.[2] A dog with mildly abnormal hips can still have a CHIC number.

That is why you should read each result, not just the number. Look at the test date, who performed it, and the actual grade. For example, OFA hip ratings range from Excellent, Good and Fair (normal) to Borderline and Mild, Moderate or Severe dysplasia.

How to verify a breeder's claims

  1. Get the parents' registered names or registration numbers, for both the mother and the father.
  2. Search the OFA database at ofa.org. Results for hips, eyes, patellas and heart appear on each dog's page, with dates.
  3. Ask for copies of any other reports, such as a respiratory grading certificate or DNA results. Check that the dog's name and microchip number match.
  4. Ask about the wider family. Have siblings, grandparents or previous litters needed airway surgery, spinal surgery or cesarean sections? Honest breeders will tell you.
  5. Meet the mother if you can. Watch her breathing at rest and after a short walk. Loud snoring while awake, gasping, or needing to stop and recover are warning signs.

Red flags that should make you walk away

  • "The parents are vet checked" with no written results.
  • Health claims that cannot be found in the OFA database.
  • Heavy marketing of "rare" colors such as merle, or "fluffy" coats, instead of health evidence.
  • Pressure to pay a deposit fast, or refusal to let you see where the puppies are raised.
  • Puppies sold before 8 weeks of age, or shipped with no chance to ask questions.
  • Parents that snore loudly at rest, struggle to breathe in mild weather, or have very narrow, slit-like nostrils.

Questions to ask, word for word

  • "Can you send me the registered names of both parents so I can look them up on OFA?"
  • "Have the parents had a breathing assessment? What grade did they get?"
  • "Which DNA tests were health tests, not color tests?"
  • "Has the mother had cesarean sections? How many litters has she had?"
  • "Have any related dogs needed airway or spinal surgery?"
  • "What happens if my puppy develops a hereditary problem?"

A good breeder will be glad you asked. Someone who becomes evasive or pushy has already answered your question.
