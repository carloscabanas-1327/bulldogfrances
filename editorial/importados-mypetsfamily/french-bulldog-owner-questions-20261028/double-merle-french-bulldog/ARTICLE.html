What is a double-merle dog?

The term generally describes a dog inheriting merle-associated variants from both parents. At the molecular level, merle alleles can differ in length, and even inconspicuous variants can matter when planning a litter.

A breeder cannot reliably exclude every merle genotype from appearance alone, especially when a pattern is cryptic or masked.

Why can hearing and vision be affected?

UC Davis describes higher risks of ocular and auditory abnormalities among double-merle dogs. These are associations with particular genetic combinations, not a prediction that every affected puppy will have the same outcome.

Eye appearance is not a complete visual assessment, and a dog responding to sound may still have hearing loss on one side.

How can problems be identified?

Discuss a specialist ophthalmic examination for suspected eye abnormalities and BAER testing for hearing. The veterinarian can evaluate whether additional assessment is warranted.

Do not use home tricks, clapping, or photos as proof of normal hearing or vision.

What should owners know about genetic results?

Ask for the laboratory report rather than just a statement that a dog is 'merle clear.' The report should identify the tested variants and be interpreted for the specific dog.

Genetic analysis is valuable, but it does not replace the ordinary clinical health screening French Bulldogs need for respiratory, cardiac, orthopedic, and ocular issues.

What if your dog has a confirmed disability?

Dogs with hearing or vision impairments can often learn reliable routines using touch cues, predictable layouts, and safe enclosed environments. Work with a veterinarian and a qualified positive-reinforcement trainer.

The responsible response is to provide safe, appropriate care, not to stigmatize a dog because of its color or disability.
