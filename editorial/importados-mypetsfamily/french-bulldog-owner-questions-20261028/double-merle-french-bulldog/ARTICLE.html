A double merle French Bulldog inherits merle-associated variants from both parents. This can increase the risk of hearing and eye defects, but outcomes vary with genotype and other genetic factors.[1]

Key takeaways

  • Double merles can result when two dogs carrying merle variants are bred, even if one has a cryptic or barely visible pattern.[1]
  • In a study across multiple dog breeds, 10 of 40 sampled double merles (25%) had hearing loss versus 3.5% of single merles. The sample does not estimate deafness rates in French Bulldogs.[2]
  • UC Davis says double merles may have hearing, eye, skeletal and other defects, and that breeding two merles is discouraged.[1]
  • Double merles can still be loving pets. They need a hearing test, an eye exam and some adapted training.

How a double merle is created

Merle is caused by an insertion in the PMEL (Silver) gene. It is incompletely dominant: one copy (single merle) usually produces the mottled pattern, and two copies (double merle) produce a much stronger effect.[1]

If both parents carry one merle-associated variant, simple Mendelian inheritance predicts roughly one in four puppies could inherit a variant from both; allele-size complexity and mosaic results require specialist interpretation. The risk is easy to miss because some merle carriers look solid. UC Davis calls these "cryptic merle" (also phantom or ghost merle) and warns they "may be misclassified as non-merles."[1] Coat color alone cannot reliably establish merle genotype; a validated PMEL/M-locus DNA test is more informative, and complex allele-size results may require guidance from the testing laboratory.[1]

How to recognize a double merle

UC Davis notes double merles "often can have an all white coat."[1] Other common signs include pale or blue eyes and eyes that look smaller than normal. Appearance alone is not proof. A mostly white Frenchie may be a piebald rather than a double merle, so a DNA test is the reliable way to know.

Health risks, with numbers

Hearing. A 2009 study tested 153 merle dogs from multiple breeds using BAER; 40 were double merles. Among those 40, 10% were deaf in one ear and 15% in both ears, compared with 2.7% and 0.9% among single merles. Participants were not a representative French Bulldog sample.[2] The Merck Veterinary Manual explains that this type of deafness develops within the first weeks of life and that affected ears are totally deaf.[3]

Eyes. UC Davis lists ophthalmologic defects among the problems seen in double merles.[1] These can range from mild changes to eyes that are abnormally small or do not see.

Other systems. UC Davis also mentions abnormalities of the skeletal, cardiac and reproductive systems.[1]

On top of this, a double merle Frenchie carries all the usual breed risks, including airway disease (BOAS), heat intolerance and spinal problems.

Testing a double merle puppy

  • BAER hearing test. Merck calls BAER the most definitive test and notes that deafness in one ear may go undetected without it.[3] Ask your vet for a referral; many veterinary schools and neurology clinics offer it.
  • Eye exam by a veterinary ophthalmologist. This shows the structure of each eye and how much vision is likely.
  • Merle DNA test. Confirms the genotype, which matters if anyone ever considers breeding the dog (they should not).

Living with a deaf or low-vision Frenchie

  • Use hand signals for sit, come, stay, good and no. Deaf dogs learn them quickly.
  • Teach positive attention cues using a hand signal or gentle light cue. If using a vibration cue, introduce it with reward-based training and professional guidance; never use electric shock or punishment.
  • Keep the dog on a leash or in a fenced area. A deaf dog cannot hear cars or your call.
  • Approach gently and wake the dog with a soft touch or by stamping lightly on the floor, so it is not startled.
  • For low vision, keep furniture in the same places, use textured mats to mark stairs and doorways, and talk to the dog as you approach if it can hear.
  • Tag the collar with "I am deaf" and your phone number.

Many rescue groups place double merle dogs and can offer practical advice.

Why responsible breeders never pair two merles

UC Davis discourages breeding two merle dogs because their offspring may inherit two merle-associated variants and develop health problems.[1] Ask for laboratory results from both parents and discuss any complex allele patterns with a veterinary genetics professional. Appearance is not a substitute for testing.
