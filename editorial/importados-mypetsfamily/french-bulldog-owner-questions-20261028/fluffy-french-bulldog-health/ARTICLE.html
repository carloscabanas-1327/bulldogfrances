Fluffy French Bulldogs have a long coat caused by a recessive gene. Here is what that gene does, and what it does not change about their health.

Key takeaways

  • A fluffy coat comes from a recessive variant of the FGF5 gene. A puppy needs two copies, one from each parent, to be fluffy.[1]
  • UC Davis identifies a specific FGF5 variant (called L4) in French Bulldogs and notes long hair "is also known as Fluffy" in the breed.[1]
  • The long coat does not fix or cause the breed's breathing problems. A fluffy Frenchie has the same short face and the same risks.
  • The AKC standard describes a short, smooth coat, so fluffy dogs cannot be shown in conformation.[2]

What makes a French Bulldog fluffy

Coat length in dogs is controlled largely by a gene called FGF5 (fibroblast growth factor 5). UC Davis Veterinary Genetics Laboratory describes five recessive FGF5 variants linked to long hair in dogs. One of them, known as L4, occurs in Afghan Hounds, Eurasiers and French Bulldogs.[1]

Because the trait is recessive, the genetics are easy to follow:[1]

GenotypeCoatCan pass on long hair?
S/SShortNo
S/LShort (carrier)Yes, to about half of puppies
L/LLong ("fluffy")Yes, to all puppies

Two short-coated carriers can produce fluffy puppies. On average, 25% of their litter would be long-haired.[1] A DNA coat-length test tells you exactly which version a dog carries.

Is a fluffy Frenchie a purebred?

The FGF5 variant can appear in dogs with only French Bulldog ancestry, so a long coat alone does not prove a dog is mixed. However, the AKC breed standard calls for a coat that is "brilliant, short and smooth" and states that "coats other than short and smooth are a disqualification."[2] A fluffy Frenchie can be registered if its parents are registered, but it does not meet the standard for the show ring.

Fluffy puppies often sell for much more than standard puppies. That price reflects rarity and demand, not better health.

Are fluffy Frenchies healthier?

We found no studies comparing the health of fluffy and short-coated French Bulldogs. The FGF5 gene is linked to coat length.[1] The features behind brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS) are different ones, such as a long soft palate and narrow nostrils.[3] Our interpretation: a long coat on its own does not make a French Bulldog breathe more easily.

French Bulldogs as a breed have much higher odds than other dogs for BOAS, ear discharge and skin fold infections, according to a large UK veterinary study.[4] A fluffy Frenchie shares the same body plan, so the same health screening matters: breathing grade, hips, eyes, patellas and heart.

Watch out for breeders who pair the fluffy gene with other "rare" traits such as merle. Merle carries its own risks of hearing and eye problems, separate from coat length.

Grooming a longer coat

  • Brush two to three times a week with a soft slicker brush, focusing on the ears, chest, legs and tail area where tangles form.
  • Keep the face folds and ears clean and dry. Long hair can trap moisture and debris.
  • Trim hair around the paws and rear to keep them clean.
  • Do not shave the coat to keep a Frenchie cool. The coat helps protect skin from sun. Focus on shade, air conditioning and short walks instead.
  • Dry thoroughly after baths. A damp undercoat close to the skin can irritate it.

Skin and coat problems to watch for

See your vet if you notice patchy hair loss, redness, odor, sores or constant scratching. In French Bulldogs these signs are often caused by allergies, parasites or skin infections, and a longer coat can hide them. Lift the hair and look at the skin during brushing so problems are caught early.

Before you buy a fluffy puppy

  • Ask for the coat-length DNA results of both parents.
  • Ask for the standard health tests and a breathing grade for both parents, not only color and coat tests.
  • Avoid sellers who combine "fluffy" with merle or other fad colors as selling points.
  • Expect the same lifelong costs and care as any French Bulldog, plus extra grooming.
