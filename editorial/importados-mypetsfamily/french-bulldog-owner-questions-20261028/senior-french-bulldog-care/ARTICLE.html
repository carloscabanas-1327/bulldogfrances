Caring for an older French Bulldog means watching breathing, spine, weight and comfort more closely. Here is a practical senior care plan.

Key takeaways

  • The American Animal Hospital Association advises vet exams at least every 6 months for senior dogs, with screening tests.[1]
  • Breathing problems from BOAS can get worse with age and extra weight.[2]
  • Back and leg weakness in older Frenchies can come from disc disease or degenerative myelopathy. Both need a vet.[3][4]
  • Small home changes, like ramps, rugs and cooler rooms, make a big difference.

When is a French Bulldog a senior?

There is no single cutoff age. Many vets begin treating a Frenchie as a senior somewhere around 8 years old, and earlier for dogs with ongoing health problems. Changes such as slowing down, graying around the muzzle, more sleep and stiffness after rest are common signs.

Note that many French Bulldogs in veterinary studies are young. In the large UK VetCompass study, the median age of French Bulldogs was just 1.5 years, compared with about 4.5 years for other dogs, because the breed's popularity rose so quickly.[5] Good data on aging Frenchies is still limited.

Vet visits and screening

AAHA's senior guidance stresses "more frequent examinations (at least every 6 mo) in conjunction with appropriate diagnostic screening tests."[1] Ask your vet about:

  • Blood and urine tests for kidney, liver and thyroid function
  • Blood pressure
  • A breathing and airway check
  • A dental exam and cleaning plan, with anesthesia managed for a flat-faced breed
  • Pain assessment for back, hips and knees
  • Eye checks for cataracts or dry eye
  • Checking any new lumps

Common problems in older Frenchies

  • Breathing and heat intolerance. BOAS signs such as noise, sleep apnea and exercise intolerance can worsen over time.[2]
  • Back problems. Disc disease can cause pain, a wobbly gait or paralysis. Cornell advises not delaying surgery when it is recommended.[3]
  • Degenerative myelopathy. A slow, painless weakness of the back legs in older dogs. A DNA test for a common variant is on the AKC panel for the breed.[4]
  • Arthritis in hips, knees and elbows.
  • Skin and ear infections, already common in the breed.[5]
  • Dental disease. Crowded teeth in a short jaw trap food.
  • Hearing and vision loss.

Diet and weight

  • Older dogs often need fewer calories. Ask your vet to set a target weight.
  • Weigh your dog monthly. Even 1 to 2 pounds matters for a small dog.
  • Extra weight makes breathing and spine problems worse.
  • Unexplained weight loss is a reason to see your vet.
  • Ask before adding joint supplements, so they fit your dog's health conditions.

Making your home senior-friendly

  • Use non-slip rugs on hard floors.
  • Add ramps or pet steps for sofas and beds.
  • Block stairs if your dog struggles with them.
  • Choose an orthopedic bed in a cool, quiet spot.
  • Keep rooms cool in summer and use air conditioning.
  • Raise food and water bowls slightly if bending down seems uncomfortable.
  • Keep walks short and frequent, at your dog's pace.

Quality of life

Keep a simple diary of good and bad days: appetite, breathing, sleep, mobility, interest in family and favorite things. When bad days start to outnumber good ones, talk honestly with your vet about pain control, comfort care and, when the time comes, end-of-life options.
