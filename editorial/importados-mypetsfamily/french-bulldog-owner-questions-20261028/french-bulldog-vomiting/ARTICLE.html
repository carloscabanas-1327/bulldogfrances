French Bulldog vomiting can look like regurgitation, but the two are different. Vomiting usually involves retching and belly contractions; regurgitation is often passive. Here is how to tell them apart, watch for emergencies and understand why breathing disorders can sometimes play a role.[1][2]

Key takeaways

  • Vomiting is forceful and involves the belly muscles. Regurgitation is passive and brings up undigested food, often tube-shaped.[1]
  • In French Bulldogs, regurgitation and gagging often go along with BOAS, the breed's airway disease.[2]
  • Call a veterinarian about recurring vomiting or any vomiting accompanied by blood, pain, weakness, fever or dehydration. Do not wait several days if your dog is worsening.[1]
  • Repeated unproductive retching with a swollen belly can be bloat, a life-threatening emergency.[3]

Vomiting or regurgitation?

According to the Merck Veterinary Manual, vomiting is an active process with abdominal contractions, often preceded by nausea, drooling or retching. Regurgitation is generally a passive return of material from the esophagus without marked abdominal effort. Regurgitated food is often undigested and can appear tube-shaped. Coughing or breathing problems may accompany regurgitation.[1]

VomitingRegurgitation
EffortHeaving, belly contractionsLittle or none, food just comes up
TimingAny time after eatingOften soon after eating or drinking
What comes upPartly digested food, liquid, bileUndigested food, often tube-shaped

Tell your vet which one you saw. A short phone video is very helpful.

Why Frenchies bring food back up

The University of Cambridge lists gastrointestinal signs among the recognized features of BOAS.[2] Cambridge notes that BOAS-affected dogs may have difficulty swallowing and regurgitation, sometimes involving reflux, esophageal changes or a hiatal hernia. Altered pressure during obstructed breathing can contribute.[2] However, vomiting or regurgitation alone cannot establish a BOAS diagnosis, and unrelated digestive disorders also occur.

Other common causes include eating too fast, eating something unusual (garbage, grass, toys), sudden diet changes, food intolerance, infections and parasites.

Repeated passive regurgitation can increase concern about aspiration. If coughing, rapid or labored breathing, unusual tiredness or fever develops after an episode, seek prompt veterinary evaluation rather than trying a home remedy.[4]

When to call the vet

Merck recommends detailed evaluation for long-term or frequent vomiting, or when it occurs with blood, abdominal pain, lethargy, dehydration, weakness, fever or weight loss.[1] Contact a veterinarian sooner if symptoms are severe or repeated, and also when:

  • Your dog is a young puppy, a senior, or has another illness
  • Vomiting and diarrhea happen together
  • Your dog will not eat or drink
  • You suspect your dog swallowed a toy, sock, bone or toxin
  • Regurgitation happens regularly, even if your dog seems well

Emergency signs

  • Bloat: a swollen, tight belly with retching that brings nothing up. Merck calls bloat "a life-threatening emergency."[3]
  • Possible blockage: repeated vomiting with lethargy and loss of appetite. Merck warns an obstructed dog "may be unable to stop vomiting, which can lead to life-threatening consequences."[3]
  • Sudden bloody vomiting or diarrhea, which can quickly lead to dehydration and shock.[3]
  • Breathing trouble after vomiting or regurgitation: inhaled food or liquid can cause aspiration pneumonia and requires urgent veterinary assessment.[4]

What you can do at home

If an otherwise alert adult dog has had a single episode and is breathing normally, contact your veterinarian for individualized advice, especially if this is unusual for your dog. While arranging advice:

  • Keep fresh water available unless your veterinarian instructs otherwise. If drinking triggers further vomiting, call your vet; dehydration can worsen quickly.[1]
  • Ask your vet before withholding food or giving any human medicine. Many human stomach remedies are unsafe for dogs.
  • Follow your veterinarian's instructions on whether and when to resume food. Do not impose an unsupervised fast on puppies or medically fragile dogs.
  • Watch closely and write down what came up and when.

Preventing repeat episodes

  • Discuss meal size and schedule with your veterinarian if episodes recur; frequent regurgitation needs diagnosis rather than only a feeding change.
  • If your dog gulps food, ask your veterinarian whether a slow feeder is appropriate. A slow feeder does not treat BOAS or esophageal disease.
  • Note whether episodes occur with exertion, excitement or immediately after eating; this timing may help your veterinarian identify the cause.
  • Avoid frequent, unexplained food changes while your veterinarian is investigating persistent symptoms.
  • Ask for an assessment of healthy body condition and breathing, especially if your Frenchie has noisy breathing or exercise intolerance.
  • If regurgitation is frequent, ask your vet about a BOAS assessment. Ask your veterinarian whether airway disease could be contributing to digestive signs; the appropriate treatment depends on a clinical evaluation.

What to tell your veterinarian

A clear description can be more useful than guessing the cause. Note how many episodes occurred, how soon after food or water they happened, and whether you saw active belly contractions or material coming up with little effort. If it is safe, record a brief video for the veterinarian rather than attempting to recreate the event.

  • Describe what came up: food that still looked undigested, fluid, bile-like material, blood or something unusual. Do not handle suspicious material without precautions.
  • Report other signs: coughing, noisy breathing, pain, unusual tiredness, diarrhea, loss of appetite or difficulty drinking. These details change the urgency.
  • List recent exposures: any new food, access to garbage, toys or medications, and possible contact with a toxin. If a toxin or foreign object is suspected, seek urgent veterinary guidance rather than waiting for another episode.
  • Give context: your dog's age, chronic conditions, current medicines and whether regurgitation has occurred before.

Merck explains that the veterinary workup may include an examination and, depending on the signs, tests of blood, urine or stool and imaging such as X-rays or ultrasound.[1] A French Bulldog that regularly regurgitates also deserves consideration of swallowing, esophageal and airway function, since Cambridge identifies gastrointestinal signs as one possible component of BOAS.[2] These observations help prioritize a professional workup; they do not establish a diagnosis from a checklist.
