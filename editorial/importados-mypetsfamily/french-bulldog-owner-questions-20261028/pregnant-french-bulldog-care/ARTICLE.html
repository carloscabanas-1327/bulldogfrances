Pregnant French Bulldog care requires a veterinary birth plan because this breed has elevated odds of difficult delivery. Discuss pregnancy confirmation, fetal monitoring and emergency access before labor begins.[2]

Key takeaways

  • Pregnancy in dogs lasts about 62 to 64 days.[1]
  • In a UK veterinary-record study, French Bulldogs had 9.13 times the adjusted odds of a dystocia diagnosis relative to other dogs (95% CI 5.17–16.13); this is not an individual birth probability.[2]
  • Plan the birth with your vet early, including whether a scheduled cesarean section is safer.
  • Pregnant dogs with brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS) may need additional breathing and heat-management precautions; assess each dog's airway with your veterinarian.[3]

Confirming the pregnancy

Pregnancy in dogs lasts about 62 to 64 days.[1] Your vet can confirm it with:

  • Ultrasound: usually from about 3 to 4 weeks after mating
  • Blood test (relaxin): generally around 30–35 days into pregnancy, depending on veterinary assessment.[1]
  • X-ray: fetal skeletons become visible after about day 45; Merck notes that radiographs after day 55 provide the most useful estimate of litter size.[1]

Week-by-week care

WeeksCare
1 to 4Normal diet and gentle walks. Ask your vet about deworming, parasite control and which medicines to avoid.
5 to 6Begin moving to a puppy or growth formula as your vet advises. Feed smaller, more frequent meals.
7 to 8Ask your veterinarian about late-pregnancy imaging, preferably timed after about day 55 when counting puppies; confirm the delivery plan and emergency clinic.
9Short, calm walks only. Keep her cool. Many vets track her temperature in the final days.

Keep her lean and comfortable. Do not give supplements such as extra calcium unless your vet prescribes them.

Why Frenchie births are high-risk

A Royal Veterinary College study found French Bulldogs had about 9 times the odds of dystocia, or difficult birth, compared with other dogs.[2] Potential contributors to difficult birth include maternal pelvic anatomy, relative fetal size or position, and poor uterine contractions. The precise cause in an individual dog needs veterinary assessment.[4]

Breathing is a second concern. Labor is hard physical work, and the breed is prone to BOAS, which causes noisy breathing and heat intolerance.[3] Anesthesia for a cesarean also needs a team experienced with flat-faced dogs.

Planning the delivery

  • Ask your vet whether they recommend a planned cesarean section, and how they will time it.
  • Find out what happens if labor starts at night or on a weekend. Save the emergency clinic number.
  • Prepare a warm, clean whelping box in a quiet room.
  • Have puppy supplies ready: towels, a heat source with a safe area to move away from it, a scale and a notebook.

Emergency signs

Call your vet or emergency clinic right away if she:

  • Has intense, continuous contractions for around 30 minutes with no puppy; call sooner if she seems distressed.[4]
  • Has dark green, blackish or foul-smelling discharge before delivery, which may indicate placental separation.[4]
  • Has an interval longer than two hours between puppies while more are expected; veterinary evaluation is especially urgent when there are contractions or distress.[4]
  • Goes beyond the delivery window estimated by her veterinarian, especially when ovulation timing or fetal monitoring raises concern. A mating date alone cannot predict the precise delivery day.[1]
  • Has breathing trouble, collapses or seems very weak

Ask your vet to give you their own thresholds in advance, since they may differ for a planned cesarean.

After the puppies arrive

  • Make sure every puppy nurses within the first hours.
  • Weigh puppies at birth and frequently during the first week, following veterinary guidance. Unexpected weight loss or failure to gain requires prompt attention.[1]
  • Check the mother's incision (after a cesarean), mammary glands and discharge every day.
  • Watch for restlessness, trembling or stiffness in the mother, which can signal low calcium (eclampsia), an emergency.
  • Keep the room warm but not hot, for the mother's breathing.
