## 1. Ear mites (Otodectes cynotis)

1. The life cycle from egg to adult takes about 3 weeks and passes through 5 stages. Adults live about 2 months and keep reproducing the whole time. The full cycle happens on the host, though mites can survive briefly in the environment. [VCA-EAR] https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/ear-mites-otodectes-in-cats-and-dogs
2. Ear mites are the second most common external parasite after fleas. They are highly contagious and spread by direct contact with an infested animal. [VCA-EAR] (same URL)
3. Ear mites are more common in puppies and kittens and more frequent in cats than dogs. They spread through close contact, including from dog to cat. Some infested animals, especially cats, show no signs. Mites can survive in the environment for several weeks. [ESCCAP] https://www.esccap.org/uploads/docs/cgqtqpf1_0720_ESCCAP_GL3__English_v19_1p.pdf
4. Signs include head shaking, constant ear scratching, and drooping of normally upright ears. Itch varies but may be severe. There is a build-up of dark brown cerumen. Severe cases can become suppurative otitis externa, with possible eardrum perforation. [MSD-MANGE] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/integumentary-system/mange/mange-in-dogs-and-cats
5. Other signs include hair loss from scratching, crusted rash, and aural hematoma. Discharge is "dark, waxy, or crusty". Secondary yeast and bacterial infections are common and should be treated. [VCA-EAR] https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/ear-mites-otodectes-in-cats-and-dogs
6. Diagnosis requires seeing the mite, either with an otoscope or by examining ear debris under a microscope. ESCCAP specifies about x40 magnification and also mentions small moving white spots in the canal. Sedation may be needed for very sore ears. [VCA-EAR] and [ESCCAP] (URLs above)
7. In the professional workup, the vet mixes cerumen with mineral oil on a slide and examines it at low power for eggs, larvae, and adults. Cytology of a stained ear swab separates the other causes: cocci (staphylococci or streptococci), rods (Pseudomonas, E. coli, Proteus), or *Malassezia* yeast on squamous cells. In cats, a dark "coffee-ground" exudate should strongly suggest *O. cynotis*. A slimy green discharge with only rods on cytology is highly suggestive of *Pseudomonas*. [MSD-OTITIS] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/ear-disorders/otitis-externa/otitis-externa-in-animals
8. Owner-level point on telling causes apart: "A dark discharge often indicates yeast or parasites but can also appear with bacterial or mixed infections." A swab smear quickly shows whether bacteria, yeast, or parasites are present. Mites are one primary cause of otitis externa, along with allergies, foreign bodies, and bacterial or yeast infection. [MSD-OWNER-OTITIS] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/ear-disorders-of-dogs/ear-infections-and-otitis-externa-in-dogs
9. Treatment options:
   - Topical selamectin and moxidectin are the approved systemic therapies.
   - Ear-canal ivermectin or milbemycin formulations are effective (in cats).
   - Ear cleaning with a ceruminolytic should be part of any treatment.
   - "Affected and in-contact animals should receive appropriate ear parasiticide treatment."
   [MSD-MANGE] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/integumentary-system/mange/mange-in-dogs-and-cats
10. VCA lists licensed options as daily topical ear medications or single-use products: Milbemite (milbemycin), Revolution (selamectin), Advantage Multi (moxidectin), Simparica (sarolaner, an isoxazoline), and Bravecto (fluralaner, an isoxazoline). Medications don't penetrate eggs or pupae, so treatment targets adults and larvae. Thorough ear cleaning improves results. The vet may re-examine the pet to confirm the mites are gone. [VCA-EAR] https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/ear-mites-otodectes-in-cats-and-dogs
11. Treat all in-contact animals in multi-animal homes and kennels. Treatment is acaricidal ear drops or a systemic spot-on or oral product, repeated as needed. [ESCCAP] (URL above). The Merck owner edition agrees: "Animals that have contact with infested dogs should also be treated." [MSD-OWNER-MITES] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/skin-disorders-of-dogs/mite-infestation-mange-acariasis-scabies-in-dogs
12. Human risk is rare. Mites may cause a temporary itchy rash in susceptible people, which resolves once the pets are cleared. [VCA-EAR]. ESCCAP calls transmission to humans rare and advises wearing gloves. [ESCCAP]
13. General mite principles (from the Sarcoptes, Notoedres and Psoroptes page, not specific to Otodectes): all animals that contacted an infested animal need treatment, and treatment should last two mite life cycles. Isoxazolines have caused neurological events, so use caution in dogs with a seizure history. Test for the ABCB1 (MDR1) mutation before ivermectin in susceptible breeds. [MSD-PINNA] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/ear-disorders/diseases-of-the-pinna/mite-infestations-affecting-the-pinna-in-animals

**Myths and misconceptions (ear mites)**
- **"Dark ear discharge means ear mites."** Merck says dark discharge often indicates yeast *or* parasites and can also appear with bacterial or mixed infections, so a vet swab is needed. [MSD-OWNER-OTITIS]
- **"Treat it at home with vinegar."** Merck says to avoid irritating home remedies like vinegar mixtures because they can worsen swelling and secretions. [MSD-OWNER-OTITIS]
- **"Ear mites are a cat problem, so only the itchy pet needs treatment."** Both Merck and ESCCAP say every in-contact animal should be treated, and some carriers (especially cats) show no signs. [MSD-MANGE], [ESCCAP]

## 2. Demodicosis (demodectic mange)

1. *Demodex canis* lives in small numbers on nearly all dogs. Puppies pick up the mites from the dam during nursing, within the first 72 hours after birth. The disease is not considered contagious. [MSD-MANGE] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/integumentary-system/mange/mange-in-dogs-and-cats
2. Demodicosis is not contagious to other animals or to humans. Exposure to an affected dog is not considered dangerous for a healthy dog. [VCA-DEMO] https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/mange-demodectic-in-dogs
3. Localized form: usually in dogs under 1 year old. It shows as 1 to 5 well-demarcated patches of hair loss, redness, and scaling, mainly around the lips, around the eyes, and on the forelimbs. Itch is absent or mild. Spontaneous recovery is frequent. [MSD-MANGE]. The owner edition says localized cases "often resolve without treatment," and a few progress to generalized disease. [MSD-OWNER-MITES] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/skin-disorders-of-dogs/mite-infestation-mange-acariasis-scabies-in-dogs
4. Localized juvenile disease often resolves in 6 to 8 weeks. Juvenile generalized demodicosis typically affects dogs from about 2 to 18 months. Adult-onset generalized disease usually appears after age 4. [ESCCAP] https://www.esccap.org/uploads/docs/cgqtqpf1_0720_ESCCAP_GL3__English_v19_1p.pdf
5. Juvenile-onset generalized disease is linked to an inherited defect in cell-mediated immunity. Adult-onset generalized disease (typically over 3 years old, occasionally from 1.5 years) is often associated with:
   - malignant lymphosarcoma
   - malignant melanoma
   - hyperadrenocorticism (Cushing's)
   - hypothyroidism
   - diabetes mellitus
   - other neoplastic or debilitating disease

   In many cases no underlying cause is found. [MSD-MANGE]
6. ESCCAP adds systemic infections and prolonged immunosuppression to the list of associated conditions, and says to avoid glucocorticoids. [ESCCAP]
7. Isoxazolines are now the preferred treatment for juvenile- and adult-onset generalized demodicosis. Use is off-label in some regions, at the labelled flea and tick dose. Monthly afoxolaner, lotilaner, or sarolaner, or fluralaner every 12 weeks, has been studied. Adult-onset cases may take longer. Continue treatment until signs resolve **and** at least two consecutive negative skin scrapings are obtained 1 month apart. [MSD-MANGE]. ESCCAP says most isoxazolines are approved for generalized demodicosis, and cure is declared only if there is no relapse within 12 months after therapy stops. [ESCCAP]
8. VCA lists isoxazolines (afoxolaner/NexGard, fluralaner/Bravecto, sarolaner/Simparica, lotilaner/Credelio) and macrocyclic lactones (moxidectin, ivermectin, milbemycin, doramectin). The product may be chosen based on ABCB1 (MDR1) mutation risk. Amitraz dips and benzoyl peroxide shampoos are now used much less. Diagnosis is by deep skin scrapings, and occasionally biopsy. [VCA-DEMO]
9. Breeding: "Dogs with juvenile-onset generalized demodicosis should not be bred," which is "the sole prophylactic measure." [MSD-MANGE]. VCA goes further: affected dogs should not be bred, the parents of an affected dog should not be bred again, and littermates should be watched. [VCA-DEMO]. ESCCAP notes heritability is not proven but strongly discourages breeding from bitches that produced affected litters. [ESCCAP]
10. **French Bulldog data (UK VetCompass, 2013):**
    - Juvenile-onset (<2 years) prevalence across all breeds was 0.48%. Adult-onset (>4 years) was 0.05%, so juvenile disease is about 10 times more common.
    - **French Bulldogs** had a juvenile prevalence of 1.88% (95% CI 1.30–2.73) and odds ratio (OR) 5.07 (3.37–7.63) vs crossbreds. There were no adult-onset cases among 295 adult Frenchies.
    - Other bull and brachycephalic breeds, juvenile onset: British Bulldog OR 11.26 (prevalence 3.56%, the highest of any breed), Staffordshire Bull Terrier OR 7.11, Shar-Pei OR 6.57, Dogue de Bordeaux OR 5.92, Pug OR 5.41 (1.91%), Boxer OR 2.04.
    - Adult onset: Shar-Pei OR 21.91, Shih Tzu 15.42, WHWT 8.34, Pug 5.42.

    [ONEILL-2019] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7003809/
11. The authors suggest juvenile disease clusters in bull-type breeds from the same genetic clade, which may point to a shared genetic basis. They present this as a hypothesis, not proven. They flag the rising popularity of French Bulldogs, Pugs, and British Bulldogs as a concern. [ONEILL-2019]
12. Relapses can occur until 12 to 18 months of age. In dogs with suppressed immunity they usually appear 3 to 6 months after treatment stops. [VCA-DEMO]

**Myths and misconceptions (demodicosis)**
- **"Mange is contagious, so keep the dog away from other dogs and people."** Demodectic mange is not contagious to other animals or humans. [VCA-DEMO], [MSD-MANGE]
- **"All mange is the same."** Demodectic and sarcoptic mange are different diseases with different causes, treatments, and prognoses. Sarcoptic mange *is* contagious. [VCA-DEMO]
- **"Mange always itches."** Demodex overgrowth typically does not itch unless there is a secondary infection. [VCA-DEMO], [MSD-MANGE]

## 3. Fleas

1. The cat flea, *Ctenocephalides felis*, is "by far the most prevalent flea on dogs and cats." [MSD-FLEA] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/integumentary-system/fleas-and-flea-allergy-dermatitis/fleas-in-dogs-and-cats
2. **About 95% of the population lives in the environment.** Adult fleas on the pet are only about 5% of the total. The other 95% are eggs, larvae, and pupae in the home. [VCA-FLEACYCLE] https://vcahospitals.com/resources/preventive-dog/parasites/flea-lifecycle. Corroborated by Purdue's Lorraine Corriveau DVM: "Only about 5 percent of the total flea population is present as adult fleas on your pet." [PURDUE] https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/releases/2015/Q2/purdue-vet-spring-signals-return-of-dangerous-pet-parasites.html. CAPC, Merck, and ESCCAP describe the immature stages as the majority but give no percentage.
3. Life-cycle stages and timing:
   - Eggs hatch in 1 to 6 days.
   - The larval stage lasts 5 to 11 days, or up to 2 to 3 weeks.
   - Pupae take 1 to 2 weeks, but adults can stay in the cocoon for up to 30 weeks.
   - The full cycle takes 12 to 14 days under ideal conditions, typically 3 to 8 weeks in homes, and up to 350 days at most.

   [MSD-FLEA]
4. Egg-laying:
   - **Merck:** egg laying begins 24 to 48 hours after the first blood meal, with a peak of 40 to 50 eggs a day and an average of 27 a day over 50 days. [MSD-FLEA]
   - **CAPC:** egg production starts within 20 to 24 hours of the first blood meal. Eggs fall off the host within hours. In temperate climates, 90 to 95% of adults emerge within 21 to 35 days. [CAPC-FLEA] https://capcvet.org/guidelines/fleas/
5. Pupae can lie dormant and emerge when vibration or CO2 signal a host. This is why fleas "appear" after a family returns from holiday or boarding. [VCA-FLEACTRL] https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/flea-control-in-dogs
6. Flea allergy dermatitis (FAD) is a hypersensitivity to antigens in flea saliva. In dogs, lesions appear on the lower back, tailhead, and caudal and inner thighs. A flea-free exam does not rule out FAD. Response to strict flea control is the diagnostic test. Intradermal and IgE serology tests are no longer recommended. Anti-inflammatories "should never be used as a substitute for flea control." [MSD-FAD] https://www.merckvetmanual.com/integumentary-system/fleas-and-flea-allergy-dermatitis/flea-allergy-dermatitis-in-dogs-and-cats
7. For FAD, treat the affected pet, **all household animals**, and the environment. Complete elimination can take up to 3 months. "Lifelong flea control is required to prevent reinfestation." [MSD-FAD]. CAPC agrees that infestations may take several months to control and that every pet in the home must be treated. [CAPC-FLEA]
8. Tapeworm (*Dipylidium caninum*): flea larvae eat tapeworm eggs, which develop into cysticercoids inside the flea. Dogs and cats are infected by swallowing fleas while grooming. [MSD-FLEA]. Children can be infected by swallowing infected fleas. [CAPC-FLEA]. ESCCAP says *D. caninum* infection strongly indicates a current or recent flea infestation. [ESCCAP]
9. Year-round prevention: CAPC recommends treating all dogs and cats year-round, for life, to stop flea populations from establishing in the home. [CAPC-FLEA]. Adult fleas survive winter on untreated hosts and urban wildlife. [MSD-FLEA]. ESCCAP says year-round control may be needed, with a peak in summer and autumn. [ESCCAP]
10. Environment control steps:
    - Wash bedding and vacuum frequently.
    - Treat carpets, cracks, and the areas under furniture, using an adulticide plus an insect growth regulator.
    - Repeat after 7 to 10 days in severe infestations.
    - Outdoors, target shaded areas rather than open lawn.

    [MSD-FLEA]. VCA adds: continue flea control on **all pets** for 3 to 6 months after an infestation, and shampoos and powders have little lasting effect. [VCA-FLEACTRL]
11. Heavy infestations can cause iron-deficiency anemia, especially in young animals. Cat fleas also transmit *Rickettsia typhi* and *R. felis* (murine typhus) and *Bartonella henselae* to people. [MSD-FLEA], [CAPC-FLEA]
12. **Permethrin and cats:** permethrin is found in many dog spot-ons, shampoos, sprays, and collars. Cats lack the protein needed to break it down. Poisoning happens when dog products are put on a cat, or when a cat grooms or shares a bed with a recently treated dog. Signs (drooling, tremors, seizures) appear within hours, or up to 36 hours. "Left untreated, permethrin poisoning is usually fatal." [PDSA] https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/pet-health-hub/conditions/permethrin-toxicity-in-cats. ESCCAP: synthetic pyrethroids labelled for dogs only can be toxic to cats. [ESCCAP]. VCA: some dog flea products are toxic to cats, so always read labels. [VCA-FLEACTRL]

**Myths and misconceptions (fleas)**
- **"I don't see fleas, so there aren't any" / "the adults are the whole problem."** Adults are only about 5% of the population, eggs are dust-sized, and larvae hide deep in carpets. [VCA-FLEACYCLE], [VCA-FLEACTRL]. A flea-free FAD dog can still be flea-allergic. [MSD-FAD]
- **"Fleas are a summer problem."** Carpets and central heating let fleas breed indoors all year, and CAPC recommends year-round prevention. [VCA-FLEACTRL], [CAPC-FLEA]
- **"The product failed, so the fleas are resistant."** VCA says there is no evidence of resistance. Failures usually come from improper application, untreated environments or pets, or reinfestation from dormant pupae. [VCA-FLEACTRL]

## Gaps and caveats
- No loaded source gives the percentage of canine otitis cases caused by Otodectes. The isoxazoline efficacy papers for ear mites (PMC5148825 afoxolaner, PMC5240227 fluralaner) were found in search, but the PMC page returned a reCAPTCHA, so no numbers were taken from them.
- CAPC's flea pages were last updated in 2017. Use Merck (2026) as the primary source for life-cycle numbers.
- The sources disagree on time to first eggs (CAPC 20–24 h, Merck 24–48 h, ESCCAP within 48 h) and on daily egg output (ESCCAP average 20, Merck average 27, all agree on a peak of 40–50). Phrase these as "within 1–2 days" and "up to 40–50 eggs a day."
- The ESCCAP GL3 PDF extract did not show the edition date. Cite it as "ESCCAP GL3, 6th ed." only after checking the PDF cover.

## 1. Kennel cough / CIRDC — verified facts

1. Main causes are *Bordetella bronchiseptica*, canine parainfluenza virus (CPIV) and canine adenovirus 2 (CAV-2). Canine influenza is also listed and distemper virus less often. Reovirus, canine herpesvirus and CAV-1 are of questionable significance, the role of *Mycoplasma* is unclear, and secondary bacteria (*Pseudomonas*, *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*) can follow. — Merck Vet Manual (prof.), Tonozzi, 2022/2024 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/respiratory-system/respiratory-diseases-of-small-animals/kennel-cough
2. Incubation: signs typically appear 5–10 days after exposure (Merck). Cornell gives 2–10 days, and about 6 days on average for *Bordetella*. UF lists ≤1 week for Bordetella, CPiV, CAV-2, H3N2 CIV and CRCoV. — Merck prof. (URL above); Cornell "Bordetellosis", Nov 20, 2025 — https://www.vet.cornell.edu/index%2Ephp/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/bordetellosis ; Cornell "The risks of kennel cough" — https://vet.cornell.edu/departments/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/risks-kennel-cough ; UF — https://ufl.pb.unizin.org/shelteranimalphysicalhealth/chapter/canine-respiratory-pathogens-and-their-properties/
3. Duration: severity usually drops during the first 5 days and the disease lasts 10–20 days. Stress can cause a relapse during recovery. — Merck prof. — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/respiratory-system/respiratory-diseases-of-small-animals/kennel-cough
4. Most mild cases resolve fully within 7–10 days (AVMA; Cornell Bordetellosis). Most dogs recover within about 2 weeks (Cornell kennel cough). — https://www.avma.org/blog/answering-clients-questions-about-cirdc ; Cornell URLs above
5. Signs: a "goose honk" cough, often followed by retching or gagging, that is easily triggered by gentle pressure on the trachea (Merck). Some dogs also sneeze, have nasal or eye discharge, mild fever, lethargy or poor appetite (Cornell Bordetellosis). — Merck prof.; Cornell Bordetellosis
6. Warning signs of bronchopneumonia: fever, purulent nasal discharge, depression, anorexia and a productive (moist) cough. Puppies are more prone to severe disease and pneumonia. Cornell adds labored breathing and high fever. — Merck prof.; Cornell "risks of kennel cough"
7. Signs that call for prompt veterinary care: a lingering cough, weakness, loss of appetite, difficulty breathing, worsening illness, or coughing severe enough to cause vomiting or breathing trouble. — AVMA@Work blog, Dec 6, 2023 — https://www.avma.org/blog/answering-clients-questions-about-cirdc
8. **Brachycephalic dogs:** AVMA lists short-nosed (brachycephalic) breeds, "such as French Bulldogs", as higher risk for CIRDC, along with very young or old dogs, immunocompromised dogs and dogs with existing respiratory or heart disease. An AVMA expert panel advised prompt veterinary care for brachycephalic dogs. — AVMA@Work blog, Dec 6, 2023 — https://www.avma.org/blog/answering-clients-questions-about-cirdc ; AVMA News, Nolen, Dec 5, 2023 — https://www.avma.org/news/making-sense-mystery-illness-found-across-us
9. Contagion: dogs can stay contagious for up to 8 weeks, possibly longer, after symptoms resolve, and *Bordetella* survives in the environment for at least 10 days (Cornell Bordetellosis). UF shelter tables give shedding of ≤3 months for Bordetella without antibiotics, under 2 weeks for CPiV, CAV-2 and CRCoV, and ≤3 weeks for H3N2 influenza. Because most viral shedding lasts under 14 days, UF calls isolation of about 2 weeks feasible. AVMA notes that dogs can be contagious while looking healthy. — Cornell Bordetellosis; UF; AVMA blog
10. Spread: through respiratory droplets, direct contact and contaminated objects such as bowls and door handles. It spreads fastest where dogs are confined close together (boarding, daycare, veterinary hospitals, shelters, groomers, dog parks). Diluted household bleach (1:32) is effective on surfaces. — Merck prof.; Cornell kennel cough; Cornell Bordetellosis
11. Vaccines: *Bordetella* vaccines come as intranasal, oral and injectable (subcutaneous) forms. AAHA 2022 classes them as **noncore** (based on lifestyle and risk): intranasal or oral is a single dose with annual boosters, and injectable is 2 doses 2–4 weeks apart, then annual. Canine influenza (H3N8/H3N2) is noncore: 2 doses 2–4 weeks apart, then annual. WSAVA 2024 says respiratory vaccines are "non-core for pet dogs living in typical homes" but should be considered core for shelter dogs, and that most non-core vaccines give about 1 year of immunity. — AAHA — https://www.aaha.org/resources/2022-aaha-canine-vaccination-guidelines/recommendations-for-core-and-noncore-canine-vaccines/ ; WSAVA 2024 — https://wsava.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/2024-Guidelines-for-the-Vaccination-of-Dogs-and-Cats.pdf ; Cornell Bordetellosis (vaccine can start at 6–8 weeks; some vets boost every 6 months for high-risk dogs)
12. Treatment: most dogs with only a cough do not need hospitalization, and antibiotics are usually not needed unless pneumonia is present (Merck). When used, doxycycline is one option. Cough suppressants (hydrocodone, butorphanol) can be used for persistent dry cough but are contraindicated in pneumonia. Cornell says steroids and NSAIDs do not shorten the illness. — Merck prof.; Cornell "risks of kennel cough"
13. **2023 US "mystery" respiratory illness:** Oregon received more than 200 case reports from mid-August 2023. Oregon's case definitions were (a) mild-to-moderate tracheobronchitis lasting 6–8 weeks or longer that responds poorly to antibiotics, (b) chronic pneumonia that responds poorly to antibiotics, and (c) acute pneumonia that becomes severe quickly, sometimes with poor outcomes within 24–36 hours. No single new pathogen was identified. Dr. Scott Weese suggested that falling vaccination rates and less socialization after COVID played a role. — AVMA News, Nolen, Dec 5, 2023 — https://www.avma.org/news/making-sense-mystery-illness-found-across-us
14. Follow-up: by February 2024, a New Hampshire lab had reported a "potential novel" Mycoplasma-like bacterium in 31 of 226 screened dogs. USDA testing had not indicated a novel pathogen, and the finding was not shown to cause disease. Colorado State saw more than double the canine pneumonia cases in Sep–Nov 2023 compared with 2022. — AVMA News, Nolen, Feb 22, 2024 — https://www.avma.org/news/questions-remain-canine-respiratory-disease-cases-fall

**Caveat:** Merck (prof.) still describes "revaccinate annually" for the DHPPi combination. This conflicts with AAHA 2022 and WSAVA 2024 for core vaccines. Use AAHA/WSAVA for schedules.

### Myths refuted (CIRDC)
- "A vaccinated dog can't get kennel cough." Cornell says the *Bordetella* vaccine, like a human flu shot, does not prevent infection but reduces severity, including pneumonia risk. — https://vet.cornell.edu/departments/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-information/risks-kennel-cough
- "Kennel cough always needs antibiotics." Merck says antibiotics are usually not needed unless pneumonia is present, and Cornell says most cases are viral. — Merck prof. and Cornell URLs above
- "The 2023 illness was a frightening new super-pathogen." AVMA says CIRDC outbreaks happen regularly, the outbreak was "not cause for alarm", and no novel pathogen was confirmed. — https://www.avma.org/blog/answering-clients-questions-about-cirdc ; https://www.avma.org/news/questions-remain-canine-respiratory-disease-cases-fall

## 2. Canine distemper — verified facts

1. Virus: canine distemper virus is a morbillivirus (family Paramyxoviridae), an enveloped, negative-sense single-stranded RNA virus with 7 lineages worldwide. — WOAH Technical Disease Card, 2019 — https://www.woah.org/app/uploads/2021/03/morbillivirus-canids-and-felidsinfection-with.pdf
2. Transmission: mainly airborne droplets from coughing, sneezing or barking, plus contact with saliva, urine or feces (Cornell). Dogs can spread the virus several days before signs appear, and that pre-symptom window can range from under a week to six weeks. Shedding usually lasts about 1 month (range 2 weeks to 3 months), and some dogs with neurological signs shed for 6–8 months. Merck says dogs can shed in saliva and feces for months even after they seem to have recovered. — Cornell — https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/canine-distemper-virus ; Merck owner — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/en-ca/dog-owners/disorders-affecting-multiple-body-systems-of-dogs/canine-distemper-hardpad-disease
3. Incubation: the first fever appears 3–6 days after infection (Merck owner). WOAH gives 3–6 days incubation. AKC CHF says signs typically appear 5–7 days after infection. — Merck owner; WOAH; AKC CHF/OSU — https://Vet.osu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/Canine Distemper Fact Sheet 20200123.pdf
4. Who is at risk: dogs aged 2–6 months and unvaccinated puppies older than 6 weeks are infected most often (AKC CHF). AVMA says puppies under 4 months and unvaccinated dogs are at highest risk. Fever reaches 39.5–41 °C (103–105 °F). — AKC CHF/OSU; AVMA — https://www.avma.org/public/PetCare/Pages/Canine-Distemper.aspx
5. Respiratory and GI signs: eye discharge is usually the first sign (AVMA). It is followed by fever, nasal discharge, coughing, lethargy, poor appetite, vomiting and diarrhea. A dry cough can become a wet cough with breathing difficulty if pneumonia develops (AKC CHF). — AVMA; AKC CHF/OSU
6. Neurological signs: muscle twitching, paralysis that starts in the hind legs, "chewing-gum" seizures, circling, head tilt and nystagmus (Merck). They can appear weeks after the initial illness (AKC CHF), or 1–3 weeks after the acute signs, and the prognosis worsens once they appear (WOAH). Nerve damage can be permanent. — Merck owner; AKC CHF/OSU; WOAH
7. "Hardpad": thickening of the skin of the footpads and nose (Merck; Cornell describes thickening or crusting). WOAH lists hyperkeratosis of the nose and footpads. Puppies may also develop permanently pitted, discolored adult teeth (AKC CHF). — Merck owner; Cornell; WOAH; AKC CHF/OSU
8. Mortality: AVMA calls distemper "often fatal", and survivors may have permanent nervous-system damage. WOAH says about half of infections in canids are subclinical or mild, but mortality "may approach 100% in unvaccinated populations" (stated for mink farms). No source I loaded gave a dog-specific case-fatality percentage. — AVMA; WOAH
9. No specific antiviral: "No drug kills the virus" (AVMA). Cornell says there is no specific cure and antivirals are not recommended. Care is supportive: antibiotics for secondary infections, fluids, anti-nausea and anti-seizure drugs, nutrition. Euthanasia may be considered for severe, progressive cases. — AVMA; Cornell; Merck owner
10. Core vaccine schedule: AAHA 2022 recommends at least 3 doses of a combination vaccine (CDV + CAV-2 + CPV ± parainfluenza) between 6 and 16 weeks, 2–4 weeks apart. A single booster follows within 1 year of the last puppy dose, then boosters every 3 years. Dogs first vaccinated after 16 weeks get 2 doses 2–4 weeks apart. — AAHA — https://www.aaha.org/resources/2022-aaha-canine-vaccination-guidelines/recommendations-for-core-and-noncore-canine-vaccines/
11. WSAVA 2024: core canine vaccines are CDV, CAV and CPV-2. Puppies get multiple doses every 2–4 weeks, "with the final dose being delivered at 16 weeks of age or older", and revaccination "at or after 26 weeks of age (rather than waiting until 12–16 months)". Modern MLV core vaccines give immunity lasting "many years" and should not be given "more frequently than necessary" to adults. (The loaded summary did not include the literal "every 3 years" wording. AAHA supports the 3-year interval.) Cornell gives 6–8 weeks start, every 3–4 weeks until 16–20 weeks, a booster at 1 year, then generally every 3 years. — WSAVA 2024 — https://wsava.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/2024-Guidelines-for-the-Vaccination-of-Dogs-and-Cats.pdf ; Cornell
12. Maternal antibodies: WSAVA 2024 says "maternally derived antibodies (MDA) interfere substantially with the efficacy of most currently available core vaccines". That is why the last puppy dose is given at 16 weeks or older. WOAH says juveniles become susceptible as maternal antibody wanes, and that maternal antibody may block the vaccine. — WSAVA 2024; WOAH
13. Wildlife reservoirs: foxes, wolves, coyotes, raccoons, skunks and ferrets can carry the virus, and pet dogs can catch it from wildlife (AVMA). AKC CHF adds otters and zoo animals. WOAH lists the susceptible families as Canidae, Felidae, Procyonidae, Mustelidae, Hyaenidae and Viverridae, plus bears, red pandas and seals. The disease does not infect people (AKC CHF). — AVMA; AKC CHF/OSU; WOAH
14. Environment: the enveloped virus survives outside the dog for no more than a few days (AKC CHF). It lasts longer in cold conditions and is inactivated by 1:30 bleach with more than 10 minutes contact (WOAH). — AKC CHF/OSU; WOAH

### Myths refuted (distemper)
- "Any eye discharge means distemper." AVMA says a weepy eye alone does not confirm distemper. — https://www.avma.org/public/PetCare/Pages/Canine-Distemper.aspx
- "Antivirals or a specific medicine can cure distemper." There is no drug that kills the virus, and Cornell says antivirals are not recommended. — AVMA; Cornell URLs above
- "A dog that looks recovered is no longer contagious." Merck says dogs can shed virus for months after they seem to have recovered, and Cornell says up to 6–8 months in dogs with neurological signs. — Merck owner; Cornell

## 1. Blindness in dogs (+ French Bulldog eye specifics)

1. Acute vision loss has 3 broad causes: opaque ocular media (diffuse corneal edema, cataract), retinal dysfunction (diffuse retinal detachment, progressive retinal degeneration, SARDS) and optic pathway disease (optic nerve lesions); ivermectin toxicity can cause retinal toxicity and central blindness in dogs. Blind dog with a normal-looking retina → refer to an ophthalmologist; ERG separates SARDS (flat) from optic-pathway disease (normal). — "Acute Vision Loss in Small Animals", Merck Veterinary Manual, Sara M. Thomasy DVM PhD DACVO (UC Davis), July 2024 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/emergency-medicine-and-critical-care/ophthalmic-emergencies-in-small-animals/acute-vision-loss-in-small-animals
2. SARDS: acute vision loss "often occurring throughout several days"; middle-aged to older dogs; spayed females overrepresented; Dachshunds, Miniature Schnauzers, Pugs and Brittany Spaniels reported more often; accompanying signs weight gain, lethargy, polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia; fundus changes may appear "within a few months"; diagnosis by flat-line ERG; "no effective treatment has been reported". — "Sudden Acquired Retinal Degeneration Syndrome in Dogs", Merck Veterinary Manual, S. M. Thomasy, July 2024 (updated Aug 2024) — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/emergency-medicine-and-critical-care/ophthalmic-emergencies-in-small-animals/sudden-acquired-retinal-degeneration-syndrome-in-dogs
3. Glaucoma affects about 1.7% of dogs in North America; sudden (closed-angle) glaucoma causes severe pain, redness, a dilated fixed pupil, corneal swelling and a firm eyeball, and any dog with glaucoma signs "should be seen by a veterinarian immediately"; open-angle glaucoma is painless and gradual. — "Glaucoma in Dogs" (Dog Owners), Merck Veterinary Manual, Kirk N. Gelatt VMD DACVO, reviewed June 2018, updated Sept 2024 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/eye-disorders-of-dogs/glaucoma-in-dogs
4. In acute glaucoma IOP is typically markedly elevated at >40–60 mm Hg; glaucoma is a common cause of irreversible blindness in dogs; breeds most often affected by primary angle-closure glaucoma: American Cocker Spaniel, Basset Hound, Chow Chow, Shar-Pei, Samoyed, Siberian Husky (French Bulldog not listed). — "Acute Glaucoma in Small Animals", Merck Veterinary Manual, S. M. Thomasy, July 2024 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/emergency-medicine-and-critical-care/ophthalmic-emergencies-in-small-animals/acute-glaucoma-in-small-animals
5. Cataracts in dogs are often inherited; other causes include diabetes, malnutrition, radiation, inflammation and trauma. Surgery works best before the cataract is fully mature and before lens protein leaks and causes inflammation; untreated cataracts need monitoring because complications such as glaucoma can occur. Cataracts should not be confused with the normal hardening of the central lens in older animals (nuclear sclerosis). — "Disorders of the Lens in Dogs", Merck Veterinary Manual, K. N. Gelatt, reviewed June 2018, updated April 2026 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/eye-disorders-of-dogs/disorders-of-the-lens-in-dogs
6. Diabetic cataract: in a 200-dog study (Beam et al., 1999), 50% of diabetic dogs had cataracts by 170 days after diagnosis, 75% by 370 days and 80% by 470 days. — "Treatment and management of canine diabetic cataract", Veterinary Practice (citing Beam S, Correa MT, Davidson MG, Vet Ophthalmol 1999) — https://www.veterinary-practice.com/article/treatment-and-management-canine-diabetic-cataract (the primary abstract https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11397260/ was blocked by captcha, so these figures come from the secondary source)
7. PRA is painless and inherited (mostly recessive); the first sign is night blindness (hesitating in dim rooms, very reflective eyes, dilated pupils); late-onset PRA usually appears at about 3–9 years; complete vision loss usually takes 1–2 years; there is no effective treatment, and antioxidant supplements have shown no measurable benefit. — "Progressive Retinal Atrophy in Dogs", VCA Animal Hospitals, T. Hunter, R. Llera, C. Yuill, updated 29 Nov 2023 — https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/progressive-retinal-atrophy-in-dogs
8. Signs that a dog is losing vision: anxiety or clinginess in new places, hesitating on stairs or before jumping on or off furniture, slower walks and play, startling easily (sometimes defensively), no longer making eye contact. Bumping into furniture may not appear until blindness is complete, and these signs can overlap with arthritis or cognitive dysfunction. Home check: drop a cotton ball and see whether the dog tracks it. — "Signs of vision loss in pets", VCA Animal Hospitals (no author or date shown) — https://vcahospitals.com/resources/conditions-dog/eyes-ears-nose/signs-of-vision-loss-in-pets
9. Adapting to blindness: if the eyes are not painful, most pets adjust well and live a fairly normal life, relying on smell and hearing; some adapt in weeks, others almost at once; "blindness is often harder on owners than on the pets". Home tips: keep furniture in place; set up a fixed "home base" for food, water and bed; gate off stairs and drops; keep the dog away from pools; use noisy or scented toys and floor-texture landmarks; put a bell on other pets; teach cues such as "stop" and "step up"; speak before touching; use a leash in unfamiliar places. Blind eyes still need checks for squinting, rubbing or discharge. — "Tips on Adjusting to Your Pet's Blindness", American College of Veterinary Ophthalmologists (ACVO), 15 Dec 2021 — https://acvo.org/tips-treatments-tricks/tips-on-adjusting-to-your-pets-blindness
10. French Bulldog JHC: the UC Davis VGL Juvenile Hereditary Cataract test (HSF4 gene) covers Boston Terrier, French Bulldog and Staffordshire Bull Terrier. Inheritance is autosomal recessive; cataracts begin before 1 year of age and progress to blindness by about 2–3 years; both sexes are affected equally. Results: N/N, N/JHC (carrier, passes the variant to 50% of offspring), JHC/JHC; carrier × carrier → 25% affected pups. An N/N dog can still get cataracts from other causes. — "Juvenile Hereditary Cataracts (JHC)", UC Davis Veterinary Genetics Laboratory — https://vgl.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/test/juvenile-hereditary-cataract
11. French Bulldog Club of America (parent club) CHIC requirements: CAER eye exam by a diplomate ACVO, registered with OFA (annual recertification recommended), plus hips, patella and cardiac. French Bulldogs must have the JHC DNA test, shown on OFA certificates as HSF4-1 (HSF4-2, adult-onset cataract, applies to other breeds). Respiratory Function Grading Scheme recommended. — "Testing & Registries", French Bulldog Club of America (no date) — https://frenchbulldogclub.org/health/testingregistries/
12. Corneal ulcers: in 104,233 UK dogs under primary care, prevalence was 0.80%; brachycephalic breeds had 11.18× the odds of crossbreds (95% CI 8.72–14.32). Breed prevalence: Pug 5.42%, Boxer 4.98%, Shih Tzu 3.45%, CKCS 2.49%, Bulldog 2.41% (French Bulldog not reported separately in the abstract). — O'Neill DG et al., "Corneal ulcerative disease in dogs under primary veterinary care in England: epidemiology and clinical management", Canine Genet Epidemiol 2017;4:5 (PubMed abstract) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28630713/
13. Brachycephalic dogs such as pugs and bulldogs are more prone to corneal ulcers because of their facial anatomy; predisposing factors include dry eye (KCS), entropion and distichiasis; signs are squinting, rubbing the eye, keeping the eye closed, discharge and a cloudy cornea; superficial ulcers usually heal in 3–5 days, but deep ulcers (descemetocele) can perforate and cause blindness. — "Corneal Ulcers in Dogs", VCA Animal Hospitals, C. Barnes, M. Weir, E. Ward, updated 25 Jul 2025 — https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/corneal-ulcers-in-dogs
14. VetCompass French Bulldog study (O'Neill, Church, Baral, Brodbelt, Packer, Canine Genet Epidemiol 2018;5:3, UK 2013 data): the most common problems include breathing problems, skin fold dermatitis, cherry eye (third eyelid prolapse), eye ulcers, patellar luxation and demodectic mange (the page gives no prevalence figures). — "French Bulldogs: Latest VetCompass breed explored", Royal Veterinary College — https://rvc.ac.uk/vetcompass/news/french-bulldogs-latest-vetcompass-breed-explored
15. Collar vs harness and eye pressure: in 51 eyes of 26 dogs, neck pressure from a collar raised IOP by a mean 7.4 mm Hg (+51.6%, P=0.001), while a harness raised it by 2.3 mm Hg (+15.8%, not significant). The authors advise a harness for dogs with weak or thin corneas or glaucoma. — Pauli AM et al., "Effects of the application of neck pressure by a collar or harness on intraocular pressure in dogs", JAAHA 2006;42(3):207-211 — https://jaaha.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/aaha/42/3/article-p207.xml

Myths refuted:
- "A cloudy-looking lens in an old dog is always a cataract": Merck separates cataract from normal age-related lens hardening (nuclear sclerosis).
- "A blind dog has a poor quality of life": ACVO says most pets with non-painful eyes adapt well and that blindness is often harder on the owner.
- "Bumping into things is the first sign": VCA says it may not appear until blindness is complete.
- "Antioxidant supplements slow PRA": VCA says they have shown no measurable benefit.
- "A clear JHC test means the dog can't get cataracts": UC Davis VGL says N/N dogs can still get cataracts from other causes.

## 2. How much dogs and puppies sleep; sleep and brachycephaly

1. Generation Pup cohort, owner-reported: total sleep per 24 h was 11.2 h (SD 2.9) at 16 weeks of age and 10.8 h (SD 2.7) at 12 months; night sleep 7.0 h at 16 weeks vs 7.3 h at 12 months; daytime sleep median 3.5 h at 16 weeks vs 3.0 h at 12 months. Puppies sleep more in the day and in total, adults more at night. — Kinsman R et al., "Sleep Duration and Behaviours: A Descriptive Analysis of a Cohort of Dogs up to 12 Months of Age", Animals (Basel) 2020;10(7):1172 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7401528
2. The same paper cites earlier work: dogs older than 1.5 years sleep about 60–80% of the night and 3–28% of the day, averaging 10.1 h per 24 h (secondary citation inside Kinsman 2020) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7401528
3. EEG study in the Phylogeny of Sleep database: dog total sleep 12.9 h/day (Wauquier A et al., "Visual and computer-based analysis of 24 h sleep-waking patterns in the dog", Electroencephalogr Clin Neurophysiol 1979;46(1):33-48). — Phylogeny of Sleep Database, Boston University — https://www.bu.edu/phpbin/sleep/record/?id=280
4. Hendricks 1987: English Bulldogs are a natural model of sleep-disordered breathing, with O2 saturation <90% for long periods, mainly in REM sleep. Daytime sleepiness: mean sleep latency was 12 min in bulldogs vs >150 min in control dogs. — "The English bulldog: a natural model of sleep-disordered breathing", J Appl Physiol 1987;63(4):1344-50 (Hendricks JC et al.; the authors were not shown on the loaded page) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3693167/
5. Niinikoski 2024: 63 dogs with valid home sleep recordings (28 brachycephalic, including 10 French Bulldogs; 35 normocephalic). Median obstructive respiratory event index was 3.8/h in brachycephalic vs 0.7/h in normocephalic dogs (ratio of geometric means 5.6, 95% CI 3.2–9.9). Excess weight (BCS >5/9) was an independent risk factor (ratio 3.37), and among brachycephalic dogs excess weight (5.02) and moderate/severe BOAS (2.49) raised the index. — Niinikoski I et al., "Evaluation of risk factors for sleep-disordered breathing in dogs", J Vet Intern Med 2024;38(2):1135-1145 — https://academic.oup.com/jvim/article/38/2/1135/8448880
6. Niinikoski 2023 (12 brachycephalic vs 12 control dogs, neckband home recording): brachycephalic dogs had a higher obstructive respiratory event index (median difference 3.5) and more snoring (estimator 34.2 percentage points); index and snore % were strongly correlated (rs=0.79). — Niinikoski I et al., "Description of a novel method for detection of sleep-disordered breathing in brachycephalic dogs", J Vet Intern Med 2023 (DOAJ record) — https://doaj.org/article/90c8a3d979524577818802da3f504fe1
7. Owner questionnaire on 100 Pugs and French Bulldogs referred for BOAS surgery: 56% had sleep problems, 88% exercise intolerance and 50% heat sensitivity (signs worse above 19 °C). — Roedler FS, Pohl S, Oechtering GU, "How does severe brachycephaly affect dog's lives?", Vet J 2013;198(3):606-610 (AGRIS abstract) — https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df02d87c7033e84bec894c
8. University of Cambridge BOAS research group: snoring and snorting "are indicators of airway obstruction"; dogs with normal airways breathe quietly; breathing difficulty and pauses in breathing during sleep are life-threatening and limit quality of life; French Bulldogs with moderate to severe nostril stenosis are about 20× more likely to develop BOAS; cyanosis or collapse needs immediate attention. — "Disease recognition by owners & veterinarians", Cambridge Veterinary School BOAS Research — https://www.vet.cam.ac.uk/boas/about-boas/recognition-diagnosis
9. UK Brachycephalic Working Group / VetCompass: noisy breathing at rest or during light exercise in flat-faced dogs is "never normal"; BOAS affects about 50% of French Bulldogs, ~60% of Pugs and ~40% of English Bulldogs; breathing problems generally worsen with age; BOAS affects sleep. — RVC VetCompass infographic (2024-11-18) — https://rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/VetCompass/Audio-visual%20resources/241118-Noisy-breathing-in-brachycephalic-flat-faced-dogs-at-rest-or-light-exercise-is-never-normal.pdf

Myths refuted:
- "Snoring is normal and cute in Frenchies": Cambridge (snoring indicates obstruction) and BWG (noisy breathing never normal).
- "A sleepy bulldog is just lazy": Hendricks 1987 found pathological daytime sleepiness (12 vs >150 min sleep latency) caused by disordered breathing at night.
- "Puppies sleep 18–20 hours a day": a figure widely repeated online. No reliable source was found; the only cohort data (Kinsman 2020) is owner-reported ~11 h/24 h at 16 weeks. Avoid quoting 18–20 h, or attribute it as a common claim.
Not verified: "sleeping sitting up / chin propped on a toy" as a BOAS sign. The Cambridge page does not mention it, so do not cite it to Cambridge.

## 3. French Bulldogs, stairs, jumping, IVDD

1. Of 343 French Bulldogs with neurological disease at a French referral school (2002–2016), 45.5% (156 dogs) had Hansen type I disc herniation, which made up 70.3% of myelopathies. Sites: 39.8% cervical (C3-C4 most often, 57.8% of cervical cases) and 60.2% thoracolumbar/lumbar. Median age 4.2 years (cervical) and 4.0 years (thoracolumbar). — Mayousse V et al., "Prevalence of neurological disorders in French bulldog: a retrospective study of 343 cases (2002–2016)", BMC Vet Res 2017 — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5497356/
2. Merck lists French Bulldogs among the breeds most commonly affected by disc disease (with Dachshunds, Beagles, Shih Tzus, Lhasa Apsos and Pekingese). Chondroid degeneration begins in the first months of life and extrusion can occur as early as 1–2 years. Recovery after surgery for a non-ambulatory small dog with intact deep pain is 85–95%; it drops to 50% when deep pain has been lost for <24 h and below 50% when lost for >24 h. — "Degenerative Diseases of the Spinal Column and Cord in Animals", Merck Veterinary Manual, William B. Thomas DVM DACVIM-Neurology, updated Oct 2026 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/nervous-system/diseases-of-the-spinal-column-and-cord/degenerative-diseases-of-the-spinal-column-and-cord-in-animals
3. Recurrence: over half of surgically treated French Bulldogs had another IVDD episode, and about 1 in 4 recurred within 12 months; young French Bulldogs are especially prone to cervical recurrence. — FECAVA news summarising Leu D, Vidondo B, Stein V et al., "Recurrence rate of intervertebral disc disease in surgically treated French Bulldogs: a retrospective study (2009–2019)", Acta Vet Scand 2023;65:3 — https://www.fecava.org/finnish-study-over-half-of-french-bulldogs-with-ivdd-experience-recurrence/ (the FECAVA headline calls it "Finnish", but the open-access PDF is hosted by Bern: https://boris.unibe.ch/178356/1/s13028-023-00667-0.pdf, which I did not load. Check the paper before calling it Finnish.)
4. VetCompass 2021 (2,781 French Bulldogs vs 21,850 other dogs): French Bulldogs had higher risk for 20 of 43 disorders, including narrowed nostrils (42.14×), BOAS (30.89×), ear discharge (14.40×), skin fold dermatitis (11.18×) and dystocia (9.13×). The abstract does not report IVDD. — O'Neill DG et al., "French Bulldogs differ to other dogs in the UK in propensity for many common disorders: a VetCompass study", Canine Med Genet 2021;8:13 (DOAJ) — https://doaj.org/article/f75c17d23b02455d982ac9861e09cce8
5. Stairs and hips in puppies: in 501 dogs from 103 litters (Newfoundland, Labrador, Leonberger and Irish Wolfhound; not French Bulldogs), using stairs from birth to 3 months was associated with a higher risk of radiographic hip dysplasia, while off-leash exercise on soft, moderately rough ground in the same period was protective. Authors' advice: keep puppies under 3 months off stairs. Observational study of large breeds only. — Krontveit RI et al., Am J Vet Res 2012;73(6):838-846 (AGRIS abstract) — https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df97c07c7033e84bee55e1
6. Evidence on stairs and jumping for IVDD is weak and partly reverse-causal: in dachshunds, the DachsLife 2015 survey found higher IVDH odds in dogs exercised <30 min a day and in dogs not allowed to jump on furniture, which the authors attribute at least partly to owners changing habits after diagnosis. In 1,964 dachshunds, IVDH prevalence was 15.9%, and neutering before 12 months raised risk (females RR 2.12, males RR 1.54). — Dorn M, Seath IJ, "Neuter status as a risk factor for canine intervertebral disc herniation (IVDH) in dachshunds", Canine Genet Epidemiol 2018;5:11 — https://cgejournal.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40575-018-0067-7
7. Dachshund Health UK (breed health group, not a vet school): in its survey, daily stair use and furniture jumping in dogs over 3 years were linked to lower IVDD odds (not causal). A Scandinavian study found moderate stair use associated with less disc calcification. Jumping down still puts sudden stress on the spine, puppies up to 12 months and dogs over 7 are at greater injury risk, and previously affected dogs should not jump off furniture. — "Jumping and Stairs", dachshund-ivdd.uk — https://www.dachshund-ivdd.uk/lifestyle-advice/jumping-stairs/
8. Harness, not collar: all dogs with (cervical) disc disease should wear a harness instead of a neck collar. Obesity and repeated trauma are risk factors; VCA states there is "no way to prevent intervertebral disc disease"; strict rest for 3–6 weeks after an episode. Hansen type I typically appears at 3–6 years. — "Cervical Intervertebral Disc Disease in Dogs", VCA, M. Weir, T. Hunter, R. Downing, updated 11 Mar 2025 — https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/cervical-intervertebral-disk-disease-in-dogs
(Harness also lowers eye-pressure spikes; see topic 1 fact 15, Pauli 2006.)

Myths refuted:
- "Stairs and jumping cause IVDD": the disease comes from genetically driven early disc degeneration in chondrodystrophic breeds (Merck). Survey data even link moderate activity to lower odds (Dorn & Seath; Dachshund Health UK). Sensible caution for puppies, seniors and previously affected dogs is still reasonable.
- "IVDD can be prevented completely with ramps": VCA says there is no way to prevent it. Ramps, gates and weight control reduce risk factors only.
- Krontveit's stairs finding applies to large breeds and hip dysplasia. Don't present it as proven for French Bulldogs or for IVDD.

## 4. Defecation frequency, stool consistency, constipation

1. "Most dogs produce feces at least once a day", often after meals. Contact a vet if there is no bowel movement 48–72 h after the last one. Signs of constipation: repeated unsuccessful attempts, circling, scooting, frequent squatting, crying out, reduced appetite, vomiting, and small amounts of liquid stool or mucus (leakage around a hard mass, often mistaken for diarrhea). Causes include bones and indigestible material, hair, low activity, pain (arthritis), pelvic injuries, prostate disease, dehydration, drugs, hypothyroidism and neurologic disease. — "Constipation in Dogs", VCA, C. Barnes, M. Weir, E. Ward, updated 30 Jul 2025 — https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/constipation-in-dogs
2. AKC Chief Veterinary Officer Dr Jerry Klein: "defecating one to two times daily is considered normal"; dogs on higher-fiber diets may go about three times a day; puppies go more often, usually after meals. No stool for more than 24–36 h is "a cause of concern"; straining, blood, vomiting or lethargy mean see a vet, and blockage is an emergency. — "How Often Should My Dog Be Pooping?", AKC, S. Patterdale, updated 9 Nov 2022 — https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/how-often-should-my-dog-be-pooping/
3. Definitions: constipation is "the infrequent or difficult evacuation of feces, which are typically dry and hard"; obstipation is intractable constipation. Classic signs are tenesmus and firm dry feces; thin or ribbon-like feces suggest prostate, mass or lymph node compression; sicker animals show lethargy, anorexia, vomiting and abdominal discomfort. Causes include hair or bone material, pain, malunited pelvic fractures, enlarged prostate, neuromuscular or spinal lesions, hypokalemia or hypercalcemia, and drugs. — "Constipation, Obstipation, and Megacolon in Small Animals", Merck Veterinary Manual, Allison Collier DVM DACVIM (OVC), June 2025 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/digestive-system/diseases-of-the-large-intestine-in-small-animals/constipation-obstipation-and-megacolon-in-small-animals
4. Constipation, straining and painful defecation are common signs of perineal hernia, seen mostly in intact male dogs aged 6–8 years. — "Disorders of the Rectum and Anus in Dogs", Merck Veterinary Manual, Stanley I. Rubin, updated Sept 2024 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/digestive-disorders-of-dogs/disorders-of-the-rectum-and-anus-in-dogs
5. Fecal score scale 1–7 (1 hardest, 7 softest), from a nonprofit referral hospital: ideal stool is firm and easy to pick up without leaving a mark (score 2; 3 also normal); score 1 = hard dry balls (constipation); scores 4–7 = progressively worse diarrhea; any noticeable change from the dog's usual stool suggests a problem. Constipation tips: consistent diet, water intake, fiber (pumpkin, psyllium, high-fiber foods); PEG (Miralax) works by pulling water into the stool and contains no fiber. — "Helping Your Pet Poop Perfectly", Animal Medical Center (New York), Dr Ann Hohenhaus, 20 May 2026 — https://www.amcny.org/blog/2026/05/20/helping-your-pet-poop-perfectly-vet-tips-for-healthy-stool/
6. The same AMC 1–7 scale with candy analogies (score 2 = "Tootsie Rolls: firm but not hard, pliable"). — "Fecal Scoring Scale for Dogs and Cats Made Sweeter", AMC, A. Hohenhaus, 22 Sep 2020 — https://www.amcny.org/?p=63959
7. Blockage is an emergency (vomiting, weakness, anorexia, bloating, abdominal pain, inability to poop). — AKC page above.

Notes and myths:
- The brief's "1–5 times a day" was not found in any reliable source. Verified sources say "at least once a day" (VCA) and "1–2 times, up to ~3 on high-fiber diets" (AKC). Use those.
- "Liquid stool means diarrhea, not constipation": VCA says liquid leaking around hard feces is often mistaken for diarrhea.
- The 1–7 fecal score scale originated with Purina (manufacturer). Cite the AMC explanation rather than the brand chart.
- Note for editors: the AKC and VCA thresholds differ (24–36 h "concern" vs call the vet at 48–72 h). Present both or use the VCA figure.

## 5. Puppy or dog not gaining weight

1. WSAVA uses a 9-point BCS scale; "the goal for most pets is a BCS of 4 to 5 of 9"; owners may think ideal-weight pets look too thin. A separate muscle condition score (palpation over temporal bones, scapulae, lumbar vertebrae and pelvis) is needed because an animal can be overweight and still be losing muscle. Nutritional screening belongs in every exam, and growing animals need more frequent monitoring. — WSAVA Nutritional Assessment Guidelines, J Small Anim Pract 2011 — https://wsava.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/WSAVA-Nutrition-Assessment-Guidelines-2011-JSAP.pdf
2. Puppies gain roughly 2–4 g/day per kg of expected adult weight during the first 5 months; growth begins to plateau after 6 months; small and medium breeds reach skeletal maturity at about 8–12 months; weigh weekly and adjust food so the puppy gains a small amount each week; growth charts are available. Feed 3 meals a day from weaning to 6 months (small breeds may need more), then 2 meals a day from 6 to 12 months. — "Feeding Practices in Small Animals", Merck Veterinary Manual, Sherry Lynn Sanderson DVM PhD DACVIM-Nutrition, updated Oct 2024 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/management-and-nutrition/nutrition-small-animals/feeding-practices-in-small-animals
3. Neonatal puppies gain 1–3 g/day per kg of anticipated adult weight (5–10% of body weight per day); weigh daily for the first 2 weeks, then every 3 days until 1 month; a loss of up to 10% of birth weight is acceptable on day 1. Reduced weight gain and failure to suckle are signs of sepsis. — "Management of the Neonate in Dogs and Cats", Merck Veterinary Manual, Autumn P. Davidson DVM (UC Davis), updated Aug 2025 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/management-and-nutrition/management-of-the-neonate/management-of-the-neonate-in-dogs-and-cats
4. Puppy growth charts were developed by the Waltham Petcare Science Institute with the University of Liverpool, Banfield and UCL (PLOS ONE, reported 5 Oct 2020). They cover five size categories, and dogs that later became underweight gained more slowly than the charts predicted. — University of Liverpool news, 5 Oct 2020 — https://news.liverpool.ac.uk/?p=95411 . Ten charts by sex and expected adult size; not suitable for dogs expected to exceed 40 kg. — C. Grant (OVC), "Puppy growth charts", Veterinary Focus 32(1), Apr 2022 (IVIS) — https://www.ivis.org/library/veterinary-focus/a-good-start-life-veterinary-focus-vol-321-apr-2022/puppy-growth-charts
5. Roundworms (Toxocara canis): transplacental infection is "the most common mode of infection" in puppies, and transmammary infection also occurs. Lack of growth and loss of condition are the first signs, with a dull coat and a pot belly. Deworm from 2 weeks of age, every 2 weeks until 3 months, then monthly until 6 months, and treat the nursing bitch at the same times. — "Roundworms in Small Animals", Merck Veterinary Manual, Andrew S. Peregrine, updated July 2026 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/digestive-system/gastrointestinal-parasites-of-small-animals/roundworms-in-small-animals
6. EPI: weight loss and loose stools despite polyphagia, with pale, voluminous, foul feces; signs appear only after about 90% or more of the exocrine pancreas is lost; diagnosed by serum TLI ≤5.5 mcg/L in dogs; pancreatic acinar atrophy mostly affects German Shepherds, rough Collies and Eurasiers. — "Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency in Dogs and Cats", Merck Veterinary Manual, Jörg M. Steiner, Sept 2025 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/digestive-system/the-exocrine-pancreas/exocrine-pancreatic-insufficiency-in-dogs-and-cats
7. Portosystemic shunt is "the most common liver defect present at birth"; signs include nervous-system disturbances and "a failure to grow and thrive" (Pugs among listed breeds). Other congenital causes of poor growth: cleft palate (milk from the nostrils; surgery best at ≥12 weeks), congenital megaesophagus (regurgitation when solids start, aspiration pneumonia risk). — "Congenital and Inherited Disorders of the Digestive System of Dogs", Merck Veterinary Manual, S. I. Rubin, updated June 2026 — https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/digestive-disorders-of-dogs/congenital-and-inherited-disorders-of-the-digestive-system-of-dogs
8. Brachycephalic GI signs: of 73 brachycephalic dogs referred for respiratory signs (49 French Bulldogs, 67%), about 74% had moderate or severe ptyalism, regurgitation or vomiting (grade 3 in 48%). Digestive and respiratory severity were significantly associated in French Bulldogs (P=0.034); 80% of dogs with grade 3 digestive signs also had grade 3 respiratory signs. — Poncet CM et al., J Small Anim Pract 2005;46:273-279 (PDF hosted by Cornell) — https://confluence.cornell.edu/download/attachments/344131056/Poncet_et_al-2005-Journal_of_Small_Animal_Practice.pdf?api=v2
9. Fading or failing neonates: lack of weight gain is usually the first sign; weigh puppies once or twice daily for the first several weeks; causes include environment, congenital defects, maternal factors and umbilical infections; see a vet right away because "problems can get worse very quickly at that young age". — "Fading Puppy Syndrome: When Puppies Fail to Thrive", University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine, A. Lamp / Dr A. Baratta-Martin, 27 Nov 2023 — https://vetmed.illinois.edu/pet-health-columns/fading-puppy-syndrome-when-puppies-fail-to-thrive/

Myths and notes:
- "An ideal-weight dog looks too thin": WSAVA notes owners often perceive BCS 4–5/9 as thin.
- "Faster growth is better": Merck says overfeeding speeds growth, which is undesirable.
- "A puppy that eats a lot can't have a medical problem": EPI causes weight loss with increased appetite (Merck).
- The WSAVA toolkit only links to its BCS and muscle condition score charts (the PDF did not reproduce the chart text). The "each BCS point above 5 ≈ 10% overweight" rule was NOT verified.
- WSAVA does not publish its own puppy growth charts; the widely used charts are Waltham's.

## 1. Separation anxiety / leaving a dog home alone

1. The RSPCA says dogs should be left alone for "no longer than four hours", so they can exercise, toilet and spend time with people. Puppies should be left for much less. Source: Training your dog to be left alone, RSPCA. https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/dogs/behaviour/alone
2. The RSPCA states that "eight out of 10 dogs" find it hard to cope when left alone, and that about half of them show no obvious signs. This is RSPCA-cited research and is not a peer-reviewed prevalence figure. Source: Training your dog to be left alone, RSPCA. https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/dogs/behaviour/alone
3. The RSPCA lists these signs:
   - Common: destructive chewing (often near exits, doors or windows), howling, barking, whining, toileting indoors, over-excitement on return, and anxiety before you leave.
   - Less common: trembling or panting, excessive drooling, self-injury such as paw licking or tail biting, pacing or circling, vomiting, and refusing food or food toys when alone.
   - Behaviour typically starts within a few to 30 minutes of the owner leaving.

   Source: Treating Separation Anxiety in Dogs, RSPCA. https://rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/dogs/behaviour/separationrelatedbehaviour/treatment
4. The RSPCA advises never punishing or scolding a dog for mess found on return. The dog won't link your anger to the earlier behaviour, and punishment links your arrival with anxiety, which makes the problem worse. Source: RSPCA (both pages above).
5. The RSPCA describes graded departure training in steps:
   - The dog stays in its bed while you are present, and you reward calm behaviour.
   - You move away and come back, rewarding the dog.
   - You increase distance and time gradually, then go out through the door and close it, varying how long you are gone.
   - If the dog reacts, you go back a step.
   - Once the dog copes with about one hour, longer absences are usually manageable.

   Source: Training your dog to be left alone, RSPCA. https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/dogs/behaviour/alone
6. The PDSA says: "Four hours is the longest that dogs should routinely be left alone for but every dog will be different". It adds that four hours "would generally be too long for a very active, young puppy". Source: Home alone – preventing separation anxiety, PDSA. https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/looking-after-your-pet/puppies-dogs/home-alone-preventing-separation-anxiety
7. The PDSA says: "Medication is often necessary alongside behavioural therapy". It can usually be stopped once the dog copes alone. The PDSA recommends desensitisation and counterconditioning with an ABTC-certified behaviourist. Source: Separation anxiety in dogs, PDSA Pet Health Hub. https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/pet-health-hub/other-veterinary-advice/separation-anxiety-in-dogs
8. The MSD Veterinary Manual gives these clinical facts on separation distress:
   - Signs usually begin within the first 15–30 minutes after departure.
   - Fluoxetine (1–2 mg/kg every 24 h) is FDA-approved for canine separation anxiety in the US.
   - Clomipramine is FDA-approved for dogs over 6 months. Both drugs are used together with a behaviour-modification plan.
   - The graduated-departure protocol should be expected to take months.
   - Positive punishment should be avoided because it causes fear and anxiety.
   - Leaving the dog alone should happen as rarely as possible during treatment.

   Source: Behavior Problems of Dogs, MSD Veterinary Manual, Stephanie Borns-Weil DVM DACVB, reviewed Sept 2025. https://www.msdvetmanual.com/behavior/behavior-of-dogs/behavior-problems-of-dogs
9. Separation anxiety compared with boredom:
   - Separation anxiety typically involves destroying the door the owner left through.
   - Boredom or play destruction targets other items (couch, papers), the dog may look calm on video, and it is common in dogs under 1 year.
   - Signs usually begin within 15–20 minutes, so about 30 minutes of video is usually enough.
   - The article states that "approximately 15-20 percent of dogs" in the US have separation anxiety.

   Source: Separation Anxiety: The Great Imitator, Part 1, AVSAB blog, Meredith Stepita DVM DACVB, 29 Jul 2019 (first published on Psychology Today in 2015). https://avsab.org/separation-anxiety-the-great-imitator-part-1-2/
10. Video is "the most effective and accurate way to tell if she has separation anxiety". Watch at least the first 30–60 minutes after departure. The article also advises: avoid punishment, avoid leaving the dog alone where possible (repeated panic makes it worse), and consult a veterinary behaviourist. Source: How to tell if your dog has separation anxiety and 5 things to do if she does, AVSAB blog, Dr. Kelly Ballantyne (veterinary behaviorist), 15 Aug 2021. https://avsab.org/how-to-tell-if-your-dog-has-separation-anxiety-and-5-things-to-do-if-she-does/
11. Dogs Trust gives these points:
   - If a dog won't eat or play with toys when alone but does when you are present, anxiety is more likely than boredom.
   - A "guilty" look is a response to the owner's body language, not guilt.
   - "There is no scientific evidence that any over the counter or non-prescription products work."
   - Once a dog is relaxed, leave it a "maximum of four hours" alone.

   Source: Separation anxiety in dogs, Dogs Trust. https://www.dogstrust.org.uk/dog-advice/training/unwanted-behaviours/separation-anxiety

Myths these sources refute:
- "The dog is getting revenge." The RSPCA says the dog is distressed, not seeking "revenge".
- "The dog looks guilty, so it knows it did wrong." Dogs Trust says the look is a reaction to the owner's body language.
- "Punishing the mess on return teaches the dog." The RSPCA, PDSA, AVSAB and MSD all say it makes anxiety worse.
- "Rescue dogs are more prone." The RSPCA says rehomed dogs are no more likely to develop the problem.
- "Let them cry it out." The RSPCA treatment page says this causes long-term stress and learned helplessness.
- "OTC calming products fix it." Dogs Trust says there is no scientific evidence.

## 2. Reward-based training / how to train a French Bulldog

1. AVSAB states that aversive methods, which "rely on application of force, pain, or emotional or physical discomfort – should not be used in canine training or for the treatment of behavioral disorders." Source: AVSAB Position Statement on Humane Dog Training, AVSAB, 2021 (PDF copy hosted by Illinois State Univ.). https://about.illinoisstate.edu/vfdouga/files/2021/08/AVSAB-Humane-Dog-Training-Position-Statement-2021.pdf
2. The same statement says: "Evidence supports the use of reward based methods for all canine training" and "There is no evidence that aversive training is necessary for dog training or behavior modification." Source: same.
3. The same statement lists the risks of aversive training: "increased anxiety and fear-related aggression, avoidance, and learned helplessness." It also states that "Aversive training has been shown to impair dogs' ability to learn new tasks." Source: same.
4. AVSAB advises against trainers who promote "dominance", "leader of the pack" or "alpha" theories. Source: same.
5. Vieira de Castro et al. (2020) studied 92 companion dogs from 7 training schools:
   - Groups: reward-based n=42, low-aversive n=22, high-aversive n=28.
   - Dogs at aversive schools showed more stress behaviours (lip-licking, yawning), more tense or low body states, more panting, and higher post-training rises in salivary cortisol.
   - High-aversive dogs were more "pessimistic" in a cognitive-bias test.
   - Conclusion: "aversive-based training methods, especially if used in high proportions, compromise the welfare of companion dogs".

   Source: Does Training Method Matter? Evidence for the Negative Impact of Aversive-Based Methods on Companion Dog Welfare, PLoS ONE 15(12): e0225023, Vieira de Castro AC, Fuchs D, Morello GM, Pastur S, de Sousa L, Olsson IAS, 2020 (Purdue summary page). https://caninewelfare.centers.purdue.edu/resource/does-training-method-matter-evidence-for-the-negative-impact-of-aversive-based-methods-on-companion-dog-welfare
   The UFAW press release confirms the 7 schools (3 reward-based and 4 aversive) and the 16 Dec 2020 publication date: https://www.ufaw.org.uk/press-releases/training-methods-do-have-an-impact-on-dog-welfare
   The PMC full text (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7743949) was blocked by a captcha and was not read.
6. Hiby, Rooney and Bradshaw (2004) surveyed 364 dog owners:
   - Methods used: 66% vocal punishment, 12% physical punishment, 60% praise, 51% food rewards.
   - Obedience correlated with the number of tasks trained with rewards (P<0.01) but not with punishment.
   - The number of problem behaviours correlated with the number of tasks trained with punishment (P<0.001).

   Source: Dog training methods: their use, effectiveness and interaction with behaviour and welfare, Animal Welfare 13(1):63–69, 2004. https://cambridge.org/core/journals/animal-welfare/article/dog-training-methods-their-use-effectiveness-&-interaction-with-behaviour-&-welfare/B219F8FBE35C05DA269D02F310E38B66
7. Dogs Trust advises:
   - Keep sessions short: "Keep training sessions short (five to 10 minutes at a time)".
   - Focus on one behaviour, then finish with a game.
   - "Only use positive reinforcement".
   - "don't physically force them".
   - If the dog struggles, go back a step.

   Source: Top 10 dog training tips, Dogs Trust. https://www.dogstrust.org.uk/dog-advice/training/techniques/top-ten-dog-training-tips
8. The RSPCA says: "All training should be reward based", and advises breaking training "up into short but regular sessions so your dog isn't overwhelmed". Source: How to Train Your Dog & Top Training Tips, RSPCA. https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/dogs/training
9. On dominance theory, AVSAB says it "should not be used as a general guide for behavior modification". Punishment "can directly exacerbate the problem by increasing the animal's fear or anxiety". Dogs may react with aggression "not because they are trying to be dominant" but out of fear. Source: AVSAB Position Statement on the Use of Dominance Theory in Behavior Modification of Animals, AVSAB, undated (cites 2009 work; PDF hosted by Ohio State Indoor Pet Initiative). https://indoorpet.osu.edu/sites/default/files/imce/Dominance_Position_Statement_download-10-3-14.pdf

Myths these sources refute:
- "You must be the alpha or pack leader." AVSAB's dominance statement refutes this.
- "Some dogs (e.g. aggressive ones) need aversives." AVSAB says there are no exceptions and that aversives are not necessary.
- "Punishment improves obedience." Hiby 2004 found no correlation with obedience but more problem behaviours.
- Rubbing the nose in accidents is covered in section 3.

## 3. House-training a puppy

1. The Royal Kennel Club gives this timing:
   - Take the puppy out "immediately after waking up".
   - After eating, puppies usually urinate within about 15 minutes and defecate within about 30 minutes.
   - Take them out after play or excitement.
   - Puppies need to go "at least every hour or two".

   Source: How do I toilet train my dog?, Royal Kennel Club. https://www.royalkennelclub.com/your-dog/dog-training/get-started/dog-training-and-games/how-do-i-toilet-train-my-dog
2. The Royal Kennel Club also advises:
   - Don't punish accidents, because the puppy may become afraid to toilet in front of you, even outside.
   - Avoid ammonia-based cleaners, which smell like urine.
   - A crate or pen can help, but very young puppies need a night-time toilet break.
   - Use a cue word.

   Source: same.
3. The PDSA advises taking the puppy out every 2–3 hours during the day, first thing in the morning, after waking, after meals and after play. It says: never punish accidents. Some puppies learn "within a few days but others might take much longer". Source: Toilet training your puppy, PDSA. https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/looking-after-your-pet/puppies-dogs/toilet-training-your-puppy
4. Humane World for Animals (formerly the Humane Society of the United States) gives these figures:
   - Rule of thumb: a puppy can hold its bladder for "about one hour for every month of age" (a 3-month-old needs to go about every 3 hours).
   - Take puppies out "at least every two hours".
   - Remove water about 2.5 hours before bedtime.
   - Most puppies can sleep about 7 hours overnight.
   - Housetraining typically takes 4–6 months.
   - Never punish. Rubbing the nose in it causes fear and slows progress.
   - Crate training is "a reliable and humane method for confinement".

   Source: How to potty train your dog or puppy, Humane World for Animals. https://www.humaneworld.org/en/resources/how-potty-train-your-dog-or-puppy
5. The ASPCA says rubbing a dog's nose in its mess can make the dog fear you. The dog can't link the punishment to soiling indoors and may learn to hide waste instead. "A dog will not learn anything by being scolded for a past accident." Interrupting with a clap mid-accident is acceptable. Source: House Training Your Dog or Puppy, ASPCA, 25 Sep 2014. https://www.aspca.org/news/house-training-your-dog-or-puppy
6. Blue Cross says rubbing the nose in mess or scolding is "extremely damaging and confusing to a puppy". It recommends cleaning with "a warm solution of biological washing powder" and avoiding ammonia-based products and bleach. Most puppies can't hold on overnight "for several months". Source: Puppy toilet training and house training, Blue Cross. https://www.bluecross.org.uk/advice/dog/behaviour-and-training/toilet-training-a-puppy
7. VCA advises using "a commercially available product that contains enzymes to degrade the urine or feces", because dogs are drawn back to soiled spots. "Do not scold or otherwise punish your dog." Rule out medical causes (kidney disease, infection, diabetes and others) if soiling starts suddenly. Source: Dog Behavior Problems – House Soiling, VCA Animal Hospitals, Ellen Lindell VMD DACVB, Debra Horwitz DVM DACVB, Gary Landsberg DVM DACVB. https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/dog-behavior-problems-house-soiling
8. Humane World says: "wash again with an enzymatic cleaner—these break down pet waste odors". Avoid ammonia and vinegar, and avoid steam cleaners, because heat "will permanently set the stain and the odor". Source: How to remove pet stains and odors, Humane World for Animals. https://www.humaneworld.org/en/resources/how-remove-pet-stains-and-odors
9. The RSPCA gives these crate rules:
   - "Crates must never be used as a punishment."
   - Introduce the crate in sessions of about 3 minutes, building to about 10 minutes after meals, then 30 minutes alone.
   - Never crate a dog all day while you work.
   - A trained adult is usually content for about 3 hours, and puppies can hold on for less.

   Source: How to Crate Train a Puppy, RSPCA. https://www.rspca.org.uk/adviceandwelfare/pets/dogs/environment/crates
10. Cornell advises crate training with positive reinforcement to help house training. Use a divider so a growing puppy has less space. Never leave a dog crated "for an extended period of time", and don't force a distressed dog to stay crated. Source: Dog crates: When to use them and how to pick one, Cornell Riney Canine Health Center. https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/dog-crates-when-use-them-and-how-pick-one
11. The Merck Veterinary Manual says puppies need to go first thing in the morning, last thing at night, after meals, after drinking, after naps, after play, and during the night when very young. Housetraining "usually takes just a few weeks with patience and consistency". Source: Puppy Care, Merck Veterinary Manual (Dog Owners), Natalie Antinoff DVM, July 2025. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/routine-care-of-dogs/puppy-care

Note: estimates of how long house-training takes vary between sources: Merck says "a few weeks", Humane World 4–6 months, and Blue Cross "several months". Present it as a range.

Myths these sources refute:
- Rubbing the nose in it: refuted by Humane World, ASPCA and Blue Cross.
- Scolding after the fact: refuted by ASPCA, VCA, PDSA and the Royal Kennel Club.
- Bleach or ammonia cleans it best: refuted by Blue Cross, the Royal Kennel Club and Humane World.
- A crate as a punishment tool: refuted by the RSPCA.

## 4. Puppy socialization

1. The MSD Veterinary Manual says the socialization period is between 3 and 12 weeks of age. Too little habituation or socialization in that period raises the risk of fear, anxiety and phobias. Source: Behavior Problems of Dogs, MSD Veterinary Manual, Borns-Weil, Sept 2025. https://www.msdvetmanual.com/behavior/behavior-of-dogs/behavior-problems-of-dogs
2. The Merck Veterinary Manual gives these details:
   - The socialization period runs from 19 days to 12 weeks; its Key Points section says 3–12 weeks.
   - Puppies should go to classes before 12 weeks, and indoor classes may start at 8 weeks.
   - Waiting until maternal antibodies wane (16–20 weeks) and vaccines are complete "misses the socialization window".
   - Habituate puppies to grooming, car rides, visitors and traffic sounds. Pair any feared stimulus with treats at low intensity.

   Source: Social Behavior of Dogs, Merck Veterinary Manual (professional), Stephanie Borns-Weil DVM DACVB, Sept 2025. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/behavior/behavior-of-dogs/social-behavior-of-dogs
3. AVSAB states: "Behavioral issues, not infectious diseases, are the number one cause of death for dogs under three years of age." It also says "The primary and most important time for puppy socialization is the first three months of life". Source: AVSAB Position Statement on Puppy Socialization, AVSAB, 2008 (PDF hosted by Ohio State). https://indoorpet.osu.edu/sites/default/files/imce/Puppy_Socialization_Position_download_-_10-4-14.pdf
4. AVSAB on classes and vaccines:
   - "Puppies should receive a minimum of one set of vaccines at least 7 days prior to the first class and a first deworming."
   - "puppies can start puppy socialization classes as early as 7-8 weeks of age."
   - They "should be kept up-to-date on vaccines throughout the class."

   Source: same. JAVMA News coverage adds that puppies should show no signs of illness: https://www.avma.org/News/JAVMANews/Pages/081001c.aspx
5. Stepita et al. (2013) found no higher parvovirus risk in vaccinated puppies that attended classes:
   - Clinic component: 1,012 puppies from 21 clinics in 4 US cities. CPV cases were 0 of 48 in class-attending puppies versus 14 of 876 in non-attending puppies.
   - Trainer component: 231 class puppies from 24 trainers, none suspected of CPV.
   - In total, none of 279 class-attending puppies were diagnosed with CPV.
   - Conclusion: vaccinated puppies in classes "were at no greater risk of CPV infection than vaccinated puppies that did not attend".

   Source: Frequency of CPV infection in vaccinated puppies that attended puppy socialization classes, J Am Anim Hosp Assoc 2013;49(2):95–100, Stepita ME et al.
   PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23325595/
   Full text: https://jaaha.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/aaha/49/2/article-p95.xml
6. Blue Cross says: "The socialisation 'window' is between three and 12 weeks." Before vaccinations are complete, carry the puppy out. Keep encounters short. Its exposure checklist covers:
   - people: adults, children, delivery workers
   - animals: other dogs, cats, livestock
   - surfaces: carpet, metal, sand, foil, grass
   - sounds: fireworks, babies, thunder, traffic
   - smells and household objects
   - short car trips
   - positive vet visits

   Source: Socialising your puppy, Blue Cross. https://www.bluecross.org.uk/advice/dog/socialising-your-puppy
7. The Royal Kennel Club advises: in the first four weeks home, the puppy should meet everything on the checklist "at least three times". Carry the puppy outside while it is not fully vaccinated, and it "should only meet dogs whose vaccination status you are 100% sure about". Separate the puppy from you with a crate or gate "at least once every day" from day one. Source: Your puppy's first weeks at home, Royal Kennel Club. https://www.royalkennelclub.com/your-dog/getting-a-dog/caring-for-a-new-pup/your-puppys-first-few-weeks-at-home/

Note: the sensitive period is ~3–12 weeks in the MSD and Merck manuals and Blue Cross. JAVMA News quoting AVSAB says "until 3 to 4 months". The often-cited "3–14 weeks" was not found on any page loaded.

Myths these sources refute:
- "Keep the puppy at home until all vaccines are done." Refuted by AVSAB, Merck and Stepita 2013.
- "Puppy classes spread parvo." Stepita 2013 found 0 of 279 class puppies infected.

## 5. Resource guarding / food aggression

1. The ASPCA lists these signs: stiffening over the bowl, gulping food, freezing, growling, staring, snapping and biting. Guarding ranges from running off with an item to "full-blown aggression". Puppies fed from a shared pan may learn to compete for food. Source: Food Guarding, ASPCA. https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/common-dog-behavior-issues/food-guarding
2. The ASPCA says: don't punish or intimidate a guarding dog, because "doing so is dangerous and unnecessary" and can make guarding worse. Don't try to "dominate" the dog by taking its food away. Never take stolen or scavenged food away. Source: same.
3. The ASPCA describes desensitization and counterconditioning in 7 stages: toss treats from a distance, move gradually closer, drop treats into the bowl, hand-feed treats near the bowl, touch the bowl, then lift it. Use high-value treats. Move to the next stage only after 10 relaxed meals in a row. Each adult in the family goes through the stages separately. Stop if the dog shows aggression. Source: same.
4. On child safety, the ASPCA calls households with children "unacceptably risky" for guarding dogs, because children miss warning signals. Nobody under 18 should do the exercises. Get professional help first if the dog has bitten. The ASPCA recommends a CAAB, a board-certified veterinary behaviorist (DACVB) or a CPDT with aggression experience. Source: same.
5. The MSD Veterinary Manual recommends:
   - Toss high-value treats as people approach the bowl.
   - Trade high-value treats for items the dog gives up voluntarily.
   - Teach "drop" on cue, starting with low-value items.
   - Feed or give chews in an out-of-the-way spot such as a crate.
   - SSRIs can reduce the underlying anxiety.
   - Avoid confrontation and punishment, because pinning or rolling the dog increases fear and aggression.

   Source: Behavior Problems of Dogs, MSD Veterinary Manual, Borns-Weil, Sept 2025. https://www.msdvetmanual.com/behavior/behavior-of-dogs/behavior-problems-of-dogs
6. McGuire (2019) gives shelter prevalence and outcomes:
   - 15.3% (161/1,051) of evaluated shelter dogs were assessed as resource guarders.
   - Return rates: 21.2% (31/146) for guarders versus 17.4% (151/870) for non-guarders, which was not significant.
   - Severe guarders were returned more often: 40% (10/25).
   - 24 of 25 severe guarders were eventually placed in a home and not returned.

   Source: Characteristics and Adoption Success of Shelter Dogs Assessed as Resource Guarders, Animals 2019;9(11):982, Betty McGuire (Cornell). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6912206
7. Mohan-Gibbons et al. (2018; the author names were not shown on the loaded page) studied 9 shelters and 14,180 dogs. Removing the food-guarding test caused no significant change in return rates for food-guarding dogs, and injuries (104 incidents in total) did not change. The authors recommend that "shelters discontinue the food guarding assessment". Source: The Impact of Excluding Food Guarding from a Standardized Behavioral Canine Assessment in Animal Shelters, Animals (Basel) 2018;8(2):27. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29419746/
8. An ASPCA summary of a 2012 study describes 96 food-guarding shelter dogs adopted out with a modification plan. Only 5% of food-guarding dogs were returned, compared with 9% of non-guarding adults. Only 34% of shelters tried to modify the behaviour. Source: Food Guarding in Shelter Dogs, ASPCApro (summarising Animals 2012). https://www.aspcapro.org/node/103721
9. Jacobs et al. (2017) surveyed 1,438 owners. Owners recognised biting aggression best (p<0.001) and were poorer at recognising subtler guarding signs such as rapid eating and avoidance. Owners with behaviour knowledge or training-class attendance did better. Source: Ability of owners to identify resource guarding behaviour in the domestic dog, Applied Animal Behaviour Science 188:77–83, 2017, Jacobs JA, Pearl DL, Coe JB, Widowski TM, Niel L (AGRIS record). https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65df98950f3e94b9e5da463c
10. Jacobs et al. (2018) asked 85 behaviour experts about terminology. Most preferred the term "resource guarding" to "possessive aggression". The authors define it as a dog using avoidance, threats or aggression to keep control of food or items when a person or animal is near. Source: Defining and Clarifying the Terms Canine Possessive Aggression and Resource Guarding: A Study of Expert Opinion, Frontiers in Veterinary Science 5, 2018. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fvets.2018.00115/text

Note: no reliable prevalence figure for pet dogs in general was found. The 15% figure is for a shelter population.

Myths these sources refute:
- "Take the bowl away to show who's boss." Refuted by ASPCA and MSD.
- "Punish growling." Refuted by ASPCA and MSD.
- "Food guarders are unadoptable or dangerous." The ASPCApro summary, Mohan-Gibbons 2018 and McGuire 2019 show low return rates and no change in injuries.

## 6. Puppy's first bath and bathing frequency

1. The PDSA says dogs "don't usually need bathing too often", because frequent washing strips natural oils. Muddy dogs can simply be rinsed in warm water. Use "a dog-specific shampoo", because human shampoos can irritate the skin. Introduce grooming young. Source: How often should you groom your pet?, PDSA. https://www.pdsa.org.uk/pet-help-and-advice/looking-after-your-pet/all-pets/grooming-pets
2. The ASPCA suggests bathing "at least once every three months". Use about 3–4 inches of lukewarm water and "a shampoo formulated for pets". Place "a large cotton ball in each ear" to keep water out, keep water out of the eyes and nose, and towel-dry thoroughly. Source: Dog Grooming Tips, ASPCA. https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/dog-grooming-tips
3. The Merck Veterinary Manual suggests bathing about once a month, depending on season, or when the coat is dirty or smelly. Source: Routine Health Care of Dogs, Merck Veterinary Manual (Dog Owners), Natalie Antinoff DVM, reviewed July 2025. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/routine-care-of-dogs/routine-health-care-of-dogs
   Note: the PDSA, Royal Kennel Club and Blue Cross say "only when needed", so present frequency as a range.
4. The Royal Kennel Club says "you should only be bathing your dog when necessary", because over-bathing can cause skin problems. "Dog shampoos are specifically designed for the pH balance of your dog's skin." Brachycephalic dogs with skin folds need daily fold cleaning, and the wrinkles must be kept dry "not only after bathing but also following drinking sessions". Source: How to groom my dog?, Royal Kennel Club. https://www.royalkennelclub.com/health-and-dog-care/health-dog-care/health/health-and-care/a-z-of-health-and-care-issues/dog-grooming/
5. Blue Cross says dogs "are better left without being washed for as long as possible". Use dog shampoo; baby shampoo is a gentler alternative. Water should be lukewarm. Keep water out of the ears and eyes. Towel-dry, then use a hairdryer on low heat and speed, and stop if the dog looks worried. Start bath training young. Source: How often should you wash your dog?, Blue Cross. https://www.bluecross.org.uk/advice/dog/wellbeing-and-care/how-often-should-you-wash-your-dog
6. VCA advises using "an approved puppy shampoo". Water should be "warmed to body temperature". Dry with a towel warmed in the dryer, and use any hairdryer on low or cool, away from the face. Source: Preparing Your Puppy for a Lifetime of Good Hygiene, VCA Animal Hospitals, Malcolm Weir DVM, Ryan Llera DVM, Lynn Buzhardt DVM. https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/preparing-your-puppy-for-a-lifetime-of-good-hygiene
7. The PDSA describes skin fold dermatitis as an infection in the warm, moist pocket between folds, where bacteria and yeast overgrow. It names short-nosed breeds with folds above the nose: the French Bulldog, British Bulldog and Pug. Signs include smell, redness, discharge, and pain when the folds are touched. Prevention is to clean and then thoroughly dry the folds regularly, for example with cotton wool pads in salt water. Source: Skin fold dermatitis in dogs (PDF), PDSA PetWise. https://www.pdsa.org.uk/media/9645/phh_dog_c_skin-fold-dermatitis.pdf
8. Banovic and Strzok (2019) say facial fold dermatitis is most often seen in French bulldogs, English bulldogs and pugs. It is caused by friction and a "moist, warm environment", and controlling excess moisture is key. Antimicrobial wipes are typically used once or twice daily. Source: Skin Fold Dermatitis (Intertrigo) in Dogs, Today's Veterinary Practice, Frane Banovic DVM PhD DECVD & Emily Strzok DVM, Nov/Dec 2019. https://todaysveterinarypractice.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2019/10/TVP-2019-1112_Skin_Fold_Dermatitis.pdf
9. RVC VetCompass lists skin fold dermatitis among the French Bulldog's notable disorders, together with breathing problems, cherry eye, eye ulcers, patellar luxation and demodectic mange. The news page gives no prevalence numbers. Source: French Bulldogs: Latest VetCompass breed explored, Royal Veterinary College, 2018 (study: O'Neill DG et al., Canine Genet Epidemiol 5:3, DOI 10.1186/s40575-018-0057-9). https://rvc.ac.uk/vetcompass/news/french-bulldogs-latest-vetcompass-breed-explored

Gap: no reputable source (vet body or charity) loaded gave a specific minimum age for a puppy's first bath. Search results were blogs and marketing only. Advise readers to ask their vet, and give the safety points instead: warm water, a warm room, drying thoroughly, puppy or dog shampoo, keeping water out of the ears, and drying the folds.

Myths these sources refute:
- "Human or baby shampoo is fine." The Royal Kennel Club, PDSA and ASPCA prefer dog shampoo for skin pH. Blue Cross accepts baby shampoo only as a gentler fallback.
- "Bathe weekly to keep the skin healthy." The PDSA and Royal Kennel Club say over-bathing strips oils and causes skin problems.

## 7. General puppy care, first weeks

1. CAPC says: "Puppies should be given anthelmintics starting at 2 weeks of age and repeating every 2 weeks". The treatment section gives pyrantel "at 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks of age", followed by monthly control. Nursing dams are treated at the same time. Puppies need fecal exams at least 4 times in the first year (2–4 in the control section), then 1–2 times a year. Toxocara is zoonotic and most common in children. Source: Ascarid guidelines, Companion Animal Parasite Council, updated 28 Feb 2025. https://capcvet.org/guidelines/ascarid/
2. An older CAPC summary (2008) says that without a monthly preventive, puppies should be dewormed at 2, 4, 6 and 8 weeks, then monthly "until the pet is 6 months old". 3. WSAVA (2024) on vaccines:
   - Core dog vaccines: canine distemper virus, canine adenovirus and canine parvovirus type 2, plus rabies where endemic.
   - Puppies get doses "every 2-4 weeks", with "the final dose being delivered at 16 weeks of age or older".
   - Revaccinate "at or after 26 weeks of age".
   - Adult core vaccines "should not be given any more frequently than necessary".

   Source: 2024 Guidelines for the Vaccination of Dogs and Cats, WSAVA Vaccination Guidelines Group. https://wsava.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/2024-Guidelines-for-the-Vaccination-of-Dogs-and-Cats.pdf
4. The Merck Veterinary Manual says puppies need vaccines "every 2–3 weeks" until about 4 months old. Keep them away from unvaccinated dogs until fully vaccinated. Fecal checks every 2–4 weeks until 2 negative tests in a row. Socialization is especially important at 2–4 months. Feeding:

   |---|---|
   | 6–12 weeks | 4 |
   | 3–6 months | 3 |
   | 6–12 months | 2 |
   | adult | 1–2 |

5. The Merck Veterinary Manual says puppies should see the vet every 3–4 weeks until 4 months old. Nearly all vets recommend distemper, parvovirus and rabies vaccines. Give year-round heartworm prevention. Source: Routine Health Care of Dogs, Merck Veterinary Manual (Dog Owners), Antinoff, reviewed July 2025. https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/routine-care-of-dogs/routine-health-care-of-dogs
6. Under UK law, a keeper must microchip a dog "older than 8 weeks" (in force since April 2016). Source: The Microchipping of Dogs (England) Regulations 2015, reg. 3, legislation.gov.uk. https://legislation.gov.uk/cy/uksi/2015/108/regulation/3/made?view=plain
   This is UK law only. For Spain or Latin America, check local law separately.
7. The Royal Kennel Club gives these hazards for puppy-proofing:

   - chemicals
   - plants, some of which are toxic
   - stairs and steep drops, which can harm developing bones
   - ponds and pools, where a puppy can drown

   The chewing stage lasts until "about 6 months of age". Keep the puppy supervised or in a "dog proof" area, and make sure the garden is secure. Source: The best home environment for your puppy, Royal Kennel Club. https://www.royalkennelclub.com/your-dog/getting-a-dog/caring-for-a-new-pup/the-best-environment-for-your-puppy

### DOT data, AVMA, USDA, heat risk

17. **DOT (via AP)**: at least 122 dog deaths linked to airline travel since May 2005, when airlines began reporting. Short-snouted breeds accounted for "roughly half the purebred dog deaths". Source: "Short-snouted dogs most likely to die on planes", CBS News / AP, 16 Jul 2010. https://www.cbsnews.com/news/short-snouted-dogs-most-likely-to-die-on-planes
18. **AVMA**: per DOT data released in 2010, about half of the 122 airline-associated dog deaths over the previous 5 years involved short-faced breeds: English Bulldogs 25, Pugs 11. Brachycephalic dogs have narrowed nostrils, an elongated soft palate and a narrowed trachea. They have trouble cooling down and can suffer airway collapse when stressed or overheated, and no one monitors them in the hold. AVMA advice: keep the dog at a healthy weight; acclimate it to the crate; ask about cabin travel; avoid extreme heat or cold; see a vet within 10 days before travel; avoid tranquilizers. Source: "Air travel and short-nosed dogs FAQ", AVMA, no date shown. https://www.avma.org/public/PetCare/Pages/Short-nosed-Dogs-and-Air-Travel-FAQs.aspx
19. **DOT reporting rule**: US carriers with scheduled passenger service and at least one aircraft with more than 60 seats must report animal incidents (death, injury, loss). They must also file annual totals of incidents and animals transported, with the 2025 report due 15 Jan 2026. The page shows no counts. Source: "Animal Incident Reporting 2026", US DOT, last updated 16 Dec 2025. https://www.transportation.gov/airconsumer/animal-incident-reporting-2026
20. **USDA APHIS**: short-nosed dogs are "susceptible to increased risk of heat stroke and breathing problems when exposed to stress or extreme heat". Dogs must be at least 8 weeks old and fully weaned to travel. Kennels need ventilation on three sides plus the door, and "Live Animal" labels. Source: "Pets on Planes Lesson 1: Prep and Receive Pets for Air Travel", USDA APHIS, last modified 25 Jan 2026. https://www.aphis.usda.gov/pet-travel/pets-on-planes/preparing-pets-air-travel
21. **Heatstroke (peer-reviewed)**: heat-related illness incidence in UK dogs was 0.04% (395 events among 905,543 dogs, 2016), with a 14.18% fatality rate. Brachycephalic skull shape was a risk factor, and the Chow Chow, Bulldog and French Bulldog were at significantly higher risk. Source: Hall EJ, Carter AJ, O'Neill DG, "Incidence and risk factors for heat-related illness (heatstroke) in UK dogs under primary veterinary care in 2016", *Scientific Reports* 10:9128 (2020), doi:10.1038/s41598-020-66015-8. Repository abstract: https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/39958
    - The related BSAVA 2020 abstract by the same authors gives brachycephalic vs mesocephalic OR 2.10 (95% CI 1.68–2.64), Bulldog OR 13.54, and Chow Chow OR 16.17 (vs Labrador). It does not give an OR for the French Bulldog. https://core.ac.uk/download/327948458.pdf

### CDC dog import rules (USA)

22. **From 1 Aug 2024**, all dogs entering the US must appear healthy, be at least 6 months old, be microchipped, and have a CDC Dog Import Form submission receipt. Source: "CDC Updates Dog Importation Regulation; New Rules will Start August 1, 2024", CDC press release, 8 May 2024. https://www.cdc.gov/media/releases/2024/s0508-Dog-Importation-Regulation.html
23. **Dogs from rabies-free or low-risk countries** (e.g. Spain): the microchip must be readable by a universal scanner. The receipt is emailed and is valid for 6 months (multiple entries from the same country) unless the dog visits a high-risk country. CDC suggests filling in the form from a few days to 6 months before travel. Source: "Entry Requirements for Dogs from Dog-Rabies Free or Low-Risk Countries", CDC, last updated 22 Jul 2024. https://www.cdc.gov/importation/dogs/rabies-free-low-risk-countries.html
24. **Form receipt**: arrives within about 15 minutes. It is valid for a single entry for dogs from high-risk countries, which also need a photo of the dog (taken within 15 days before arrival if the dog is under 1 year old). Source: "CDC Dog Import Form and Instructions", CDC, page dated 28 Apr 2026. https://www.cdc.gov/importation/dogs/dog-import-form-instructions.html
25. **Unvaccinated dogs**: a dog that has been in a high-risk country in the past 6 months and is not vaccinated against rabies will not be allowed in. Source: "Bringing a Dog into the U.S.", CDC, last reviewed 1 Aug 2024. https://www.cdc.gov/importation/dogs/index.html
26. **Lufthansa** also states that the US dog rules from 1 Aug 2024 require dogs to be at least 6 months old. https://lufthansa.com/es/en/register-animal-cargo-hold

- Weight including carrier: 8 kg (Iberia, Lufthansa, KLM); 10 kg (Air Europa, with the pet itself ≤8 kg); 9 kg (Aeroméxico). US carriers (AA, Delta) set size limits rather than weight limits.
- Soft carrier size: 46 x 28 x 28 cm / 18 x 11 x 11 in (AA, Delta); 45 x 35 x 25 cm (Iberia); 40 x 30 x 30 cm (Air Europa); 46 x 28 x 24 cm (KLM); 55 x 40 x 23 cm (Lufthansa).

## Topic 2: Temperament and children

1. **AKC standard, temperament**: "Well behaved, adaptable, and comfortable companions with an affectionate nature and even disposition; generally active, alert, and playful, but not unduly boisterous." Source: "Official Standard of the French Bulldog", AKC, approved 10 Jun 1991, effective 31 Jul 1991. https://cdn.akc.org/FrenchBulldog.pdf
2. **AKC standard, weight**: "Weight not to exceed 28 pounds; over 28 pounds is a disqualification." Same source. https://cdn.akc.org/FrenchBulldog.pdf
3. **FCI standard**: behaviour/temperament "Sociable, lively, playful, possessive, and keen companion dog". Weight: males 9–14 kg, females 8–13 kg. Source: FCI Standard No. 101, "Bouledogue français", official version published 10 Aug 2023. https://www.fci.be/nomenclature/standards/101g09-en.pdf
4. **AKC breed page**: the Frenchie has an "even disposition" and is "playful, alert, adaptable, and completely irresistible". It is "one of the world's most popular small-dog breeds, especially among city dwellers". Source: "French Bulldog", AKC. https://www.akc.org/dog-breeds/french-bulldog/
5. **AKC on children**: "French Bulldogs are good around young children due to their size and lack of aggression". Frenchies are described as "smart, social, and affectionate". The article says they can live with single adults, couples or families. Source: "Is the French Bulldog the Right Dog Breed for You?", Micaela Erickson, AKC, updated 3 Feb 2025. https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/dog-breeds/french-bulldog-right-for-you/
6. **Same AKC article**: "Short walks or playtime outside each day should be enough". Frenchies are "less tolerant of heat and strenuous exercise" and "aren't a particularly vocal breed". They "can be stubborn, so they may need more training than other breeds". https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/dog-breeds/french-bulldog-right-for-you/
7. **AKC on apartments (general only)**: "Generally speaking, smaller dog breeds can make good companion dogs for apartments or condominiums." The French Bulldog entry does not mention apartments. Source: "Best Dogs For Apartments", AKC Staff, updated 26 Jun 2023. https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/lifestyle/best-dogs-for-apartments/
8. **French Bull Dog Club of America (AKC parent club)**: good with kids "with proper early socialization. Both parties need to learn the rules!" Bred to be companions, they "do not do well when left alone for extended periods of time". They are "not very tolerant of heat" and should be indoor dogs. Source: "Is a French Bulldog right for you?", FBDCA Public Relations Committee. https://frenchbulldogclub.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/right-for-you-Logo-PR.pdf
9. **AVMA bite numbers**: "More than 4.5 million people are bitten by dogs each year in the United States". Children account for at least half of bite injuries. Severe bites to young children more often affect the head and neck. Source: AVMA press release "National Dog Bite Prevention Week (April 12–18): AVMA emphasizes protecting children through education and supervision", 9 Apr 2026. https://www.avma.org/news/press-releases/national-dog-bite-prevention-week-april-12-18-avma-emphasizes-protecting
10. **AVMA advice in the same release**: supervise young children around dogs, including familiar family pets. Teach children to ask the owner first and let the dog approach. Pet the back or shoulders and avoid the face, tail and ears. Don't disturb a dog that is sleeping, eating or caring for puppies. Watch for growling, a stiff posture or the dog moving away. (Same URL.)
11. **AVMA "Preventing dog bites"**: "About half of dog bite victims are children." Never leave a baby or small child alone with a dog. "Always supervise young children when around dogs, no matter how well behaved either are." Teach toddlers to handle pets gently. Consider waiting until children are older than 4 before adopting a dog. Source: AVMA, page dated 2026. https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/dog-bite-prevention
12. **CDC**: children face a higher bite risk than adults, and their injuries are often more severe. "Most dog bites affecting young children occur during everyday activities and while interacting with familiar dogs." "Do not let young children play with dogs without supervision… This includes your family pet dog." "Nearly 1 in 5 people bitten by a dog requires medical attention." Source: "Dogs" (Healthy Pets), CDC, last updated 15 Jun 2026. https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-pets/about/dogs.html
13. **Company and boredom**: RSPCA advises leaving dogs "for no longer than four hours so they have opportunities to exercise, go to the toilet, and spend time with people". Stuffed Kongs or treat balls give mental stimulation and prevent boredom. Distressed dogs "may bark, howl, toilet indoors or be destructive when left alone", or show trembling, pacing and whining. Source: "Learning to be left alone", RSPCA leaflet, 2015. https://www.rspca.org.uk/webContent/staticImages/Pdfs/LearningToBeLeftAlone.pdf
